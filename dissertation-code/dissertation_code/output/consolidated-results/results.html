Results

Descriptive Statistics and Bivariate Correlations

Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations for all study variables are presented in Tables 1 through 4. In MIDUS 2 (N = 1,289; Table 1), participants reported moderate levels of negative work-to-family spillover (M = 10.42, SD = 2.67) and negative family-to-work spillover (M = 8.31, SD = 2.34), with positive spillover scores somewhat higher for both family-to-work (M = 13.91, SD = 2.62) and work-to-family (M = 11.70, SD = 2.63) directions. The mean BTACT composite score was 0.33 (SD = 0.88). The sample was restricted to respondents who reported being currently employed. Descriptive statistics for MIDUS 3 (N = 729; Table 2) were similar, with slightly lower mean executive functioning (M = 0.18, SD = 0.61), consistent with the longitudinal aging of the MIDUS cohort.

Bivariate correlations (Tables 3 and 4) indicated that executive functioning was weakly correlated with the spillover outcomes. In MIDUS 2, executive functioning was significantly correlated with negative work-to-family spillover (r = .055, p < .05), negative family-to-work spillover (r = .081, p < .01), and positive work-to-family spillover (r = .058, p < .05), though all associations were small in magnitude. In MIDUS 3, the only significant zero-order correlation between executive functioning and a spillover variable was with negative work-to-family spillover (r = .094, p < .05). Across both waves, job demands and family demands showed the strongest associations with spillover, and the three social support variables were intercorrelated and associated with lower negative and higher positive spillover.

Analytic Approach

A series of multiple linear regression models were estimated to test all hypotheses and research questions. Two waves of the MIDUS study were analyzed separately to assess replicability: MIDUS 2 (N = 1,289) and MIDUS 3 (N = 729). All models controlled for job demands, family demands, household income, household size, number of other household members, age, gender (where not serving as a moderator), and education. Gender was coded such that 0 = male and 1 = female. Both unstandardized (B) and standardized (β) coefficients are reported, along with 95% confidence intervals and exact p-values. Results are interpreted with attention to effect sizes and consistency across waves, not solely p-values. Significant interactions were probed by computing simple slopes of executive functioning at conditional values of the moderator: the two gender categories for the categorical moderator, and one standard deviation below the mean, the mean, and one standard deviation above the mean for the continuous social support moderators. Results below are organized by outcome — within each section MIDUS 2 is reported first, then MIDUS 3.

Cross-Wave Replicability (OSC, 2015)

To formally assess replicability, the Open Science Collaboration (2015) criteria were applied to evaluate whether effects that reached significance in MIDUS 2 replicated in MIDUS 3 (Table 45). Of the two executive functioning–related terms that reached significance in MIDUS 2 — the EF conditional main effect and the EF × Coworker Support interaction, both for negative work-to-family spillover — neither showed the same sign in MIDUS 3 (0/2, 0.0%), and neither reached significance in MIDUS 3 with the same sign (0/2, 0.0%). The significant effects observed in the primary analyses did not replicate across waves.

Summary

Across all models, the most consistent predictors of work–family spillover were job demands (positively related to negative spillover) and family demands (positively related to negative family-to-work spillover and negatively related to positive family-to-work spillover). Social support variables (supervisor, coworker, and spousal) demonstrated significant main effects in expected directions across most models, even though they did not consistently moderate the executive functioning–spillover relationship. The primary finding is that executive functioning, as measured by the BTACT composite, did not demonstrate robust main or interactive effects on work–family spillover. Of the 12 directional hypotheses, three (H6, H9, and one component of RQ4) received partial support in the form of significant interactions in a single wave that did not replicate in the other; the remaining hypotheses were not supported. The significant interactions, while individually interpretable, did not replicate across waves, which limits confidence in their robustness.
