CHAPTER 5

Results


Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations for all study variables are presented in Tables 1 through 4. In MIDUS 2 (N = 932; Table 1), participants reported moderate levels of negative work-to-family spillover (M = 10.53, SD = 2.66) and negative family-to-work spillover (M = 8.43, SD = 2.32), with positive spillover scores somewhat higher for both family-to-work (M = 13.84, SD = 2.61) and work-to-family (M = 11.64, SD = 2.53) directions. The mean BTACT composite score was 0.38 (SD = 0.87). Mean household income was approximately $102,350 (SD = $57,548), and the average age was 48.91 years (SD = 8.04). The sample was 54% female. Descriptive statistics for MIDUS 3 (N = 507; Table 2) were similar, with slightly lower mean executive functioning (M = 0.23, SD = 0.60), higher household income (M = $132,684, SD = $71,978), and an older sample (M = 55.19 years, SD = 6.88), consistent with the longitudinal aging of the MIDUS cohort.

Bivariate correlations (Tables 3 and 4) indicated that executive functioning was largely uncorrelated with the spillover outcomes. In MIDUS 2, the only significant zero-order correlation between executive functioning and a spillover variable was with positive work-to-family spillover (r = .077, p < .05). In MIDUS 3, no significant correlations emerged between executive functioning and any spillover outcome. Across both waves, job demands and family demands showed the strongest associations with spillover, and the three social support variables were intercorrelated and associated with lower negative and higher positive spillover.

A series of multiple linear regression models were estimated to test all hypotheses and research questions. Because the primary sampling unit in MIDUS is the individual respondent, standard regression models were used. Two waves of the MIDUS study were analyzed separately to assess replicability: MIDUS 2 (N = 932) and MIDUS 3 (N = 507). All models controlled for job demands, family demands, household income, household size, number of other household members, age, gender (where not serving as a moderator), and education. Both unstandardized (B) and standardized (β) coefficients are reported. Results are interpreted with attention to effect sizes and consistency across waves, not solely p-values.

Hypotheses 1 through 4 predicted that executive functioning would be negatively related to negative spillover (H1: negative work-to-family; H2: negative family-to-work) and positively related to positive spillover (H3: positive work-to-family; H4: positive family-to-work). Results for these main effect models are presented in Tables 5 and 6.

In MIDUS 2 (Table 5), the main effect models explained substantial variance in negative spillover (R² = .30 for negative work-to-family; R² = .27 for negative family-to-work) but considerably less in positive spillover (R² = .04 for positive work-to-family; R² = .07 for positive family-to-work). Executive functioning was not significantly related to negative work-to-family spillover (B = −0.085, β = −0.028, SE = 0.093, p > .05), failing to support H1. Executive functioning was also not significantly related to negative family-to-work spillover (B = 0.046, β = 0.017, SE = 0.083, p > .05), failing to support H2. Executive functioning was not significantly related to positive work-to-family spillover (B = 0.173, β = 0.060, SE = 0.104, p > .05), failing to support H3. However, contrary to H4, executive functioning was significantly and negatively related to positive family-to-work spillover (B = −0.222, β = −0.074, SE = 0.105, p < .05), indicating that higher executive functioning was associated with less positive family-to-work spillover—the opposite of the hypothesized direction. Among the control variables, job demands was the strongest predictor of negative work-to-family spillover (β = .405, p < .001) and family demands was the strongest predictor of negative family-to-work spillover (β = .407, p < .001). Education was positively associated with both forms of positive spillover.

In MIDUS 3 (Table 6), the pattern was similar. Models explained more variance in negative spillover (R² = .37 for negative work-to-family; R² = .32 for negative family-to-work) than positive spillover (R² = .05 for positive work-to-family; R² = .09 for positive family-to-work). Executive functioning was not significantly related to any of the four spillover outcomes (all ps > .05), providing no support for H1 through H4. The strongest predictors of spillover remained job demands and family demands, consistent with MIDUS 2.

In summary, H1 through H4 were not supported. Executive functioning did not demonstrate the expected main effects on work-family spillover in either wave, with the exception of a small, significant effect on positive family-to-work spillover in MIDUS 2 that was in the opposite direction from what was hypothesized and did not replicate in MIDUS 3.

Hypotheses 5 and 6 predicted that gender would moderate the relationship between executive functioning and negative spillover, such that the negative relationship would be stronger for women. Research questions 1 and 2 explored whether gender moderated the relationship between executive functioning and positive spillover. Results are presented in Tables 7 and 8.

In MIDUS 2 (Table 7), the EF × Gender interaction was not significant for any of the four spillover outcomes: negative family-to-work (B = −0.035, β = −0.020, p > .05), negative work-to-family (B = 0.267, β = 0.135, p > .05), positive family-to-work (B = −0.005, β = −0.003, p > .05), or positive work-to-family (B = 0.195, β = 0.104, p > .05). H5 and H6 were not supported, and RQ1 and RQ2 revealed no significant gender moderation of positive spillover.

In MIDUS 3 (Table 8), the EF × Gender interaction was again not significant for any spillover outcome (all ps > .05). The conditional main effect of executive functioning on positive family-to-work spillover reached significance in this model (B = −1.132, β = −0.261, p < .05), though this isolated finding was not observed in MIDUS 2 and should be interpreted cautiously given the nonsignificant interaction. In summary, gender did not moderate the relationship between executive functioning and spillover in either wave.

Hypotheses 7 and 8 predicted that supervisor support would buffer the negative relationship between executive functioning and negative spillover. Research questions 3 and 4 explored whether supervisor support moderated the relationship between executive functioning and positive spillover. Results are presented in Tables 9 and 10.

In MIDUS 2 (Table 9), the EF × Supervisor Support interaction was not significant for negative family-to-work spillover (B = 0.051, β = 0.213, p > .05) or negative work-to-family spillover (B = 0.005, β = 0.019, p > .05), failing to support H7 and H8. The interaction was significant for positive family-to-work spillover (B = −0.085, β = −0.320, p < .05; RQ3), suggesting that the association between executive functioning and positive family-to-work spillover varied as a function of supervisor support. The negative interaction coefficient indicates that at higher levels of supervisor support, the positive relationship between executive functioning and positive family-to-work spillover was attenuated. The interaction was not significant for positive work-to-family spillover (B = −0.064, β = −0.246, p > .05; RQ4). Supervisor support demonstrated significant main effects across all four spillover outcomes in the expected directions.

In MIDUS 3 (Table 10), the EF × Supervisor Support interaction was not significant for negative family-to-work (B = 0.085, β = 0.260, p > .05) or negative work-to-family spillover (B = −0.006, β = −0.015, p > .05), again failing to support H7 and H8. The interaction was not significant for positive family-to-work spillover (B = 0.061, β = 0.162, p > .05), meaning the MIDUS 2 finding for RQ3 did not replicate. However, the interaction was significant for positive work-to-family spillover (B = 0.235, β = 0.605, p < .001; RQ4), indicating that supervisor support strengthened the positive association between executive functioning and positive work-to-family spillover. The conditional main effect of executive functioning was also significant in this model (B = −2.596, β = −0.584, p < .01), though this should be interpreted in light of the significant interaction, as it represents the effect of executive functioning when supervisor support is at its mean.

In summary, H7 and H8 were not supported in either wave. There was some evidence that supervisor support moderated the relationship between executive functioning and positive spillover (RQ3 and RQ4), though these effects did not replicate consistently across waves: the RQ3 interaction was significant only in MIDUS 2 and the RQ4 interaction only in MIDUS 3.

Hypotheses 9 and 10 predicted that coworker support would buffer the negative relationship between executive functioning and negative spillover. Research questions 5 and 6 explored whether coworker support moderated the relationship between executive functioning and positive spillover. Results are presented in Tables 11 and 12.

In MIDUS 2 (Table 11), the EF × Coworker Support interaction was not significant for any of the four spillover outcomes: negative family-to-work (B = 0.003, β = 0.008, p > .05), negative work-to-family (B = 0.109, β = 0.268, p > .05), positive family-to-work (B = −0.102, β = −0.257, p > .05), or positive work-to-family (B = −0.047, β = −0.121, p > .05). Coworker support had significant main effects in the expected directions, negatively predicting negative spillover and positively predicting positive spillover.

In MIDUS 3 (Table 12), the EF × Coworker Support interaction was again not significant for any spillover outcome (all ps > .05). Coworker support maintained significant main effects on positive spillover in both directions. In summary, H9 and H10 were not supported, and RQ5 and RQ6 revealed no significant coworker support moderation effects in either wave.

Hypotheses 11 and 12 predicted that spousal support would buffer the negative relationship between executive functioning and negative spillover. Research questions 7 and 8 explored whether spousal support moderated the relationship between executive functioning and positive spillover. Results are presented in Tables 13 and 14.

In MIDUS 2 (Table 13), the EF × Spousal Support interaction was not significant for any of the four spillover outcomes (all ps > .05). Spousal support demonstrated significant main effects, most notably a strong positive association with positive family-to-work spillover (β = .372, p < .001) and a negative association with negative family-to-work spillover (β = −.109, p < .001). In MIDUS 3 (Table 14), the EF × Spousal Support interaction was again not significant for any spillover outcome (all ps > .05). Spousal support maintained its strong main effect on positive family-to-work spillover (β = .487, p < .001) and was also positively associated with positive work-to-family spillover (β = .139, p < .01). In summary, H11 and H12 were not supported, and RQ7 and RQ8 revealed no significant spousal support moderation effects in either wave.

To formally assess replicability, the Open Science Collaboration (2015) criteria were applied to evaluate whether effects that reached significance in MIDUS 2 replicated in MIDUS 3 (Table 15). Of the three executive functioning–related terms that reached significance in MIDUS 2 (the EF main effect on positive family-to-work spillover, the EF × Supervisor Support interaction on positive family-to-work spillover, and the EF main effect on positive work-to-family spillover in the supervisor support model), only one showed the same sign in MIDUS 3 (33%), none reached significance in MIDUS 3 with the same sign (0%), and the one effect that was significant in both waves had non-overlapping confidence intervals and opposite signs (0% CI overlap). These results indicate that the few significant effects observed in the primary analyses did not replicate across waves.

Across all models, the most consistent predictors of work-family spillover were job demands (positively related to negative spillover) and family demands (positively related to negative family-to-work spillover and negatively related to positive family-to-work spillover). Social support variables—supervisor, coworker, and spousal—demonstrated significant main effects in expected directions across most models, even though they did not consistently moderate the executive functioning–spillover relationship. The primary finding is that executive functioning, as measured by the BTACT composite, did not demonstrate robust main or interactive effects on work-family spillover. None of the 12 directional hypotheses received consistent support across waves. Several isolated significant effects emerged but none replicated across both time points, limiting confidence in their robustness.

APPENDIX

Multiverse Analyses


To assess the robustness of the primary findings, a multiverse analysis was conducted in which the executive functioning–spillover relationship was estimated across several alternative analytic specifications. These specifications included: (a) models with no covariates, (b) models with demographics only (age, gender, education), (c) models with education as an SES indicator, (d) models examining interactions between executive functioning and each covariate (age, education, income, job demands, family demands), and (e) models relaxing the inclusion criterion to require only the respondent (rather than both partners) to be employed. Results are presented in Appendix Tables A1 through A4, with cross-wave replicability assessed in Appendix Table A5.

In MIDUS 2 (Appendix Tables A1 and A3), two specifications yielded significant effects: when no covariates were included, executive functioning was positively related to both negative family-to-work spillover (B = 0.166, p < .05) and positive work-to-family spillover (B = 0.166, p < .05). However, these effects disappeared once any control variables were introduced. Additionally, the job demands × EF interaction was significant for negative family-to-work spillover (B = 0.664, p < .05). No other specifications produced significant EF effects.

In MIDUS 3 (Appendix Tables A2 and A4), no specifications produced significant main effects of executive functioning on any spillover outcome. Several covariate × EF interactions reached significance: the job demands × EF interaction for negative work-to-family spillover (B = 1.903, p < .01), the family demands × EF interactions for negative work-to-family (B = 0.965, p < .05) and negative family-to-work spillover, and the education × EF interaction for negative family-to-work spillover (B = −1.040, p < .05). Notably, these significant interactions in MIDUS 3 did not correspond to significant effects in MIDUS 2.

Cross-wave replicability of the multiverse results (Appendix Table A5) was evaluated using the same OSC (2015) criteria applied to the primary analyses. Of the three specifications that produced significant effects in MIDUS 2, two showed the same sign in MIDUS 3 (66.7%), but none reached significance with the same sign in the second wave (0%). No effects were significant in both waves, so the confidence interval overlap criterion was not applicable. These results reinforce the primary finding that executive functioning does not have a robust or replicable relationship with work-family spillover across the analytic decisions examined in this multiverse.
