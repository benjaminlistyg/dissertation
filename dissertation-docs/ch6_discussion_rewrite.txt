CHAPTER 6

Discussion

	Neurodiversity in the workplace has received increased attention in the organizational sciences (Lefevre-Levy et al., 2023). While much of this focus has examined behavior within the work domain, it is important to recognize that the boundaries between work and non-work are permeable and subject to carry-over effects when transitioning between domains. This research sought to elucidate the relationship between executive functioning and work-family spillover, as well as examine the moderating effects of gender and social support on this relationship. This research is particularly important given that neurocognitive deficits represent the most common type of disability among working adults in the United States (CDC, 2023). Across 40 regression models tested in two independent samples of employed respondents from the Midlife in the United States study (MIDUS II: N = 1,289; MIDUS III: N = 729), the results overwhelmingly did not support the hypothesized relationships between executive functioning and work-family spillover. The findings, their implications for theory and practice, and the boundary conditions of this research are discussed below.

Summary of Findings

	Hypotheses 1 through 4 predicted that executive functioning would be negatively related to negative spillover (H1: work-to-family; H2: family-to-work) and positively related to positive spillover (H3: work-to-family; H4: family-to-work). None of the main effect tests across either wave reached statistical significance. In MIDUS II, standardized coefficients for executive functioning ranged from beta = -.053 (positive family-to-work, p = .085) to beta = .026 (positive work-to-family, p = .399). In MIDUS III, standardized coefficients ranged from beta = -.047 (positive family-to-work, p = .230) to beta = .043 (negative work-to-family, p = .173). All observed effects were well below the minimum detectable effect sizes established in the sensitivity power analysis (beta approximately .10 for MIDUS II; beta approximately .12 for MIDUS III), suggesting that any true main effect of executive functioning on spillover is likely trivially small.

	Hypotheses 5 and 6 predicted that gender would moderate the relationship between executive functioning and negative spillover, and research questions 1 and 2 explored gender moderation for positive spillover. In MIDUS II, none of the gender x executive functioning interactions were statistically significant, though the interaction for negative work-to-family spillover approached significance (B = 0.273, beta = .136, p = .054). In MIDUS III, the EF x Gender interaction was significant for negative family-to-work spillover (B = -0.556, beta = -.213, p = .019), indicating that the association between executive functioning and negative family-to-work spillover differed by gender. However, this effect was not significant in MIDUS II (B = 0.082, beta = .046, p = .523) and was in the opposite direction, providing no consistent evidence for H5 or H6. RQ1 and RQ2 also revealed no replicable gender moderation of positive spillover.

	The social support moderation hypotheses (H7-H12) and research questions (RQ3-RQ8) examined whether supervisor, coworker, and spousal support buffered the relationship between executive functioning and spillover. Of the 24 interaction tests across two waves, only two reached statistical significance, and neither replicated. In MIDUS II, the executive functioning x coworker support interaction was significant for negative work-to-family spillover (B = 0.122, beta = .300, p = .014), suggesting that the association between executive functioning and negative work-to-family spillover varied as a function of coworker support. However, this interaction was not significant in MIDUS III (B = -0.072, beta = -.113, p = .407) and was in the opposite direction. In MIDUS III, the EF x supervisor support interaction was significant for positive work-to-family spillover (B = 0.163, beta = .427, p = .007), but this effect was not significant in MIDUS II (B = -0.039, beta = -.145, p = .193) and was again in the opposite direction. No spousal support interactions were significant in either wave.

	Cross-wave replicability, assessed using the Open Science Collaboration (2015) criteria, was poor. Of the two executive functioning-related terms that reached significance in MIDUS II (the conditional main effect of executive functioning and the EF x Coworker Support interaction, both for negative work-to-family spillover in the coworker moderation model), neither showed the same directional sign in MIDUS III (Step 1: 0/2, 0.0%), and neither reached significance in MIDUS III with the same sign (Step 2: 0/2, 0.0%). The multiverse analyses confirmed this pattern: across alternative specifications--including models with no covariates, demographics only, education as SES proxy, covariates modeled as moderators, and dual-earner couple subsamples--the executive functioning coefficient was overwhelmingly nonsignificant, and the few significant effects that emerged were inconsistent across waves and specifications. Taken together, the pattern of results suggests that the few significant effects observed are likely Type I errors, consistent with what would be expected by chance given the number of tests conducted.

Theoretical Implications

	The null main effects are notable given the theoretical rationale for expecting executive functioning to play a role in managing work-family boundaries. Conservation of resources theory (Hobfoll, 1989) suggests that individuals with greater cognitive resources should be better equipped to manage cross-domain demands, and work-family border theory (Clark, 2000) posits that effective boundary management requires the kinds of planning, inhibition, and cognitive flexibility that executive functions support. However, the present findings suggest that the general cognitive capacities captured by the BTACT composite may not operate as the proximal mechanisms through which individuals manage work-family spillover.

	First, the BTACT measures a broad composite of cognitive abilities including working memory, category fluency, processing speed, and task switching. While these abilities are related to the executive functioning construct, they may be too distal from the day-to-day self-regulatory processes that govern spillover experiences. The specific cognitive mechanisms theorized to matter most for work-family management--such as emotion regulation, goal prioritization, and attentional control in the face of competing demands--may not be well captured by a telephone-administered cognitive battery. Diamond (2013) and Hofmann et al. (2012) distinguish between foundational executive capacities and the downstream self-regulatory applications of those capacities; the present findings suggest it is the latter that may be more directly relevant to spillover.

	Importantly, the ways in which low executive functioning manifests in everyday work and family life may not be well captured by the discrete, structured tasks that comprise the BTACT. In daily life, executive functioning difficulties often present as challenges with planning and sequencing multi-step activities (e.g., coordinating childcare logistics while preparing for a work deadline), difficulty disengaging from one role's demands when transitioning to another (e.g., ruminating about a workplace conflict during family dinner), poor time estimation and chronic lateness, trouble prioritizing competing obligations, and emotional reactivity under stress that spills across domains. These real-world manifestations involve complex, context-dependent interactions among cognitive, emotional, and motivational processes that unfold over extended time periods. The BTACT's constituent tasks--such as recalling digit strings backward, generating words within a category, or completing number series--are brief, decontextualized cognitive exercises that assess the capacity for executive control under standardized conditions but may not reflect how effectively individuals deploy that capacity amid the messy, emotionally charged demands of managing work and family roles simultaneously. This gap between laboratory-assessed capacity and real-world executive functioning performance has been noted in the neuropsychological literature more broadly (Chaytor & Schmitter-Edgecombe, 2003; Sbordone, 2008).

	Relatedly, the clinical conditions most strongly characterized by executive dysfunction--particularly attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)--have very low base rates in the MIDUS sample. ADHD prevalence among adults is estimated at 4-5% in the general population (Kessler et al., 2006), and this rate is likely even lower among the MIDUS participants who completed both the survey and cognitive assessments, given that study participation itself requires the organizational and follow-through capacities that individuals with significant executive dysfunction may struggle with. The resulting restriction of range at the lower end of the executive functioning distribution means that the sample largely captures normal variation in cognitive ability among relatively high-functioning adults, rather than the kinds of clinically meaningful executive deficits that theories of cognitive resource depletion and boundary management failure most directly implicate. Prior research linking executive dysfunction to adverse outcomes in adjacent domains--such as the associations between ADHD and labor market difficulties (Fletcher, 2014; Kupper et al., 2012) or family conflict (Eakin et al., 2004)--has typically examined individuals with diagnosed conditions, not the subclinical variation present in a community sample. The absence of significant effects in the present study may therefore reflect a floor effect: executive functioning may only meaningfully predict spillover when it falls below a functional threshold that few MIDUS participants reach.

	Second, the between-person design of this study may obscure within-person processes that are more relevant to spillover. Work-family spillover is inherently a dynamic, episodic process that unfolds within days and across role transitions. An individual's average level of executive functioning, measured at a single time point, may not capture the momentary fluctuations in cognitive resources that drive spillover experiences on a given day.

	Third, the null findings may reflect the strength of situational and contextual factors relative to individual cognitive differences in determining spillover. Job demands (beta = .39-.54 for negative work-to-family spillover) and family demands (beta = .35-.43 for negative family-to-work spillover) were far more potent predictors of spillover than individual differences in executive functioning across all models. This is consistent with a situation strength perspective (Mischel, 1977; Meyer et al., 2010), wherein strong situational demands constrain the influence of individual differences on behavior and outcomes. Executive functioning may matter more in weak situations where individuals have greater latitude in how they manage boundaries, a possibility that the present between-person design could not test.

	The largely null and inconsistent gender moderation results contribute to a growing body of evidence suggesting that the predictors of work-family spillover may be more similar across genders than different (Shockley et al., 2017). The one significant EF x Gender interaction--for negative family-to-work spillover in MIDUS III--did not replicate in MIDUS II, where the interaction coefficient was in the opposite direction, providing no reliable evidence that gender amplifies the consequences of executive functioning for spillover. While gendered disparities in work-family experiences certainly exist, these disparities may be driven more by differential exposure to demands and resources--such as structural inequities in household labor distribution and workplace flexibility--than by differential vulnerability to cognitive resource limitations.

Practical Implications

	This research has significant implications for individuals' overall well-being and their ability to manage work-family boundaries. The original motivation for this study was that individuals with strong executive functioning might experience less spillover due to more efficient cognitive resource allocation, better multitasking, and more effective decision-making across domains. However, the results suggest that individual differences in broad executive functioning do not meaningfully predict spillover. Instead, the practical implications of this research derive primarily from identifying which factors did consistently predict spillover across both waves and all models.

	The consistent, large effects of job demands on negative work-to-family spillover (beta = .39-.54) suggest that organizations seeking to reduce spillover should focus on workload management. Redesigning jobs to manage workload intensity--through strategies such as realistic workload expectations, adequate staffing, and autonomy over task scheduling--may be among the most effective interventions. Similarly, the strong effects of family demands on negative family-to-work spillover (beta = .35-.43) underscore the value of organizational policies that accommodate family responsibilities, such as flexible scheduling, dependent care support, and family leave policies.

	At the organizational level, the findings underscore the importance of social support in work-family dynamics. The main effects of supervisor support, coworker support, and spousal support were consistent with prior literature across both waves: each was negatively associated with negative spillover and positively associated with positive spillover. These findings are consistent with French et al.'s (2018) meta-analytic findings and suggest that social support operates as a direct resource for managing work-family demands. Organizations seeking to reduce work-family spillover and foster a more supportive work environment should consider investing in supervisor training for family-supportive behaviors (Hammer et al., 2011), fostering supportive team norms, and creating structures that facilitate coworker support. Furthermore, human resource practices such as job crafting, role design, or i-deals could be tailored to address the situational factors--demands and support--that this study identified as the primary drivers of spillover, rather than targeting individual cognitive characteristics.

	For organizations concerned about supporting employees with executive functioning difficulties or neurocognitive disabilities, the present findings suggest that cognitive screening or cognitive training programs are unlikely to meaningfully reduce work-family spillover. Instead, the environmental supports already identified in the neurodiversity literature may be more productive. Flexible work arrangements, such as remote work options and adjustable schedules, accommodate the unique needs of employees with executive functioning challenges while benefiting all employees. Assistive technologies and tools to support task management, time tracking, and goal setting can help individuals with executive functioning difficulties stay organized and focused. Implementing mentorship programs and fostering a supportive and inclusive workplace culture can also contribute to the success of employees with diverse cognitive abilities. Notably, these policies would not only benefit those with executive functioning difficulties but all employees holistically, as they promote a more accommodating and empathetic work environment (Lefevre-Levy et al., 2023). The key insight from the present study is that these environmental and structural supports are likely effective not because they compensate for cognitive deficits that drive spillover, but because they directly address the situational demands and support deficits that actually do.

Limitations and Future Directions

	Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the BTACT is a telephone-administered cognitive assessment designed for population-level research, and while it has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties (Lachman et al., 2014), it may lack the sensitivity to capture the specific executive functioning processes most relevant to work-family boundary management. As discussed above, the gap between what the BTACT measures and how executive functioning difficulties actually manifest in the daily management of work and family roles may be substantial. Future research would benefit from measures that more closely approximate the real-world cognitive demands of boundary management, such as performance-based assessments that simulate competing work and family task demands or ecological momentary assessment of cognitive load during role transitions.

	Second, the cross-sectional, between-person design of this study is poorly suited to capturing the dynamic processes through which executive functioning may influence spillover. Work-family spillover unfolds within days and across role transitions; an individual's average level of executive functioning assessed at a single time point may bear little resemblance to the momentary cognitive resource fluctuations that shape whether a difficult workday spills over into a tense evening at home. Within-person diary designs offer a particularly promising alternative. Daily diary or experience sampling studies could assess morning cognitive functioning (e.g., via brief mobile assessments of working memory or attentional control), daily work and family demands, and end-of-day spillover, allowing researchers to test whether within-person days when an individual experiences greater cognitive depletion are also days when spillover is more pronounced. This approach would disentangle whether executive functioning matters for spillover as a stable individual difference--which the present study suggests it does not--or as a fluctuating daily resource, which remains an open question. Such designs could also capture the temporal dynamics of spillover, such as whether cognitive depletion at the end of the workday predicts negative mood or conflict during the evening, and whether recovery processes (e.g., sleep, leisure) restore cognitive resources that buffer against spillover the following day.

	Third, the null findings in this study may partly reflect the limitations of quantitative survey methods for understanding a phenomenon that is experientially rich and context-dependent. Qualitative research designs could provide critical insight into the specific incidents and processes through which executive functioning difficulties lead to spillover in everyday life. Critical incident interviews with individuals who have diagnosed executive functioning difficulties (e.g., ADHD) or their partners could illuminate the concrete moments--the forgotten school pickup, the inability to disengage from a work problem during family time, the emotional outburst triggered by accumulated cognitive overload--that quantitative spillover scales may aggregate away. Such research could identify the specific executive processes most relevant to boundary management failures, inform the development of more ecologically valid measures, and generate hypotheses about when and for whom executive functioning is most consequential for spillover. Qualitative approaches could also surface compensatory strategies that individuals with executive functioning challenges use to manage spillover, which may obscure the EF-spillover relationship in cross-sectional quantitative designs if those strategies are effective.

	Fourth, the sample was restricted to respondents who were employed at the time of data collection, which ensures relevance to the work-family interface but limits generalizability to individuals who are unemployed, on leave, or out of the labor force. The MIDUS sample is predominantly White and middle-aged, further limiting generalizability to more diverse populations. Additionally, the mean household income in the analytic samples (M = $94,810 in MIDUS II; $122,059 in MIDUS III) indicates a relatively advantaged sample, which may attenuate effects if financial stress is a mechanism through which cognitive limitations influence spillover.

	Fifth, listwise deletion resulted in analytic samples substantially reduced from the full MIDUS samples. The analytic samples of 1,289 (MIDUS II) and 729 (MIDUS III) provided adequate power to detect standardized coefficients of beta approximately .10 and beta approximately .12, respectively. Given that the observed main effects were consistently smaller than these thresholds (|beta| <= .053), the null findings are unlikely to be attributable to insufficient power alone, though it remains possible that very small true effects exist below the study's detection threshold.

	Sixth, all spillover measures were self-report, raising the possibility that shared method variance or differences in self-insight could influence the observed relationships. Individuals with lower executive functioning may also have less accurate self-perceptions of their spillover experiences, which could attenuate observed associations. Multi-source designs incorporating partner or supervisor reports of spillover behaviors could address this limitation.

	The examination of boundary conditions in this study was intended to shed light on the role that one's work and home environment play in enhancing positive spillover and mitigating negative spillover. While the moderation hypotheses were largely not supported, the consistent main effects of social support across models and waves provide clear evidence that these environmental factors matter. Beyond the qualitative and within-person approaches described above, future research should also consider examining specific facets of executive functioning (e.g., inhibitory control, working memory updating) rather than the composite; investigating whether executive functioning moderates the effectiveness of boundary management strategies rather than directly predicting spillover; and examining populations with clinically significant executive dysfunction (e.g., individuals with ADHD) where effects may be more pronounced than in a community sample with a relatively restricted range of functioning.

Conclusion

	This study represents, to my knowledge, the first direct empirical examination of the relationship between executive functioning and work-family spillover. Despite strong theoretical rationale for expecting executive functioning to predict spillover outcomes, the results across two independent samples, 40 primary models, and comprehensive multiverse analyses indicate that the general executive functioning composite measured by the BTACT does not meaningfully predict negative or positive spillover between work and family domains. The few significant moderation effects that emerged--involving gender, coworker support, and supervisor support--were each isolated to a single wave and outcome and did not replicate, while spousal support showed no moderation in either wave. The findings underscore the primacy of environmental factors--particularly job demands, family demands, and social support--in shaping work-family spillover experiences. By understanding these factors, organizations can develop targeted interventions and support systems to help employees maintain a healthy work-life balance, reduce turnover and absenteeism, and enhance overall employee engagement and job satisfaction, ultimately contributing to a healthier and more productive workforce. While the null findings for executive functioning do not preclude the possibility that more specific or proximally measured cognitive processes relate to spillover, they provide an important corrective to the assumption that broad cognitive ability differences are a primary driver of work-family outcomes.
