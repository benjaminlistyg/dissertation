CHAPTER 1

Introduction

The modern workplace has experienced numerous changes in recent years, making the boundaries between one’s work and personal life increasingly blurred. This interplay between work and non-work has been a topic of interest in the organizational sciences given the negative and positive outcomes that can result from employees simultaneously juggling their professional responsibilities and non-work obligations (Allen et al., 2020; Hetrick et al., 2023). Work-to-family and family-to-work spillover are common phenomena that describe how experiences and responsibilities in one domain (e.g., one’s work or family life) both positively and negatively affect the other (Grzywacz & Marks, 2000). Spillover between domains has been shown to predict a wide range of outcomes for both workers and organizations, such as job satisfaction (Lourel et al., 2009), marital quality (Sandberg et al., 2013), employee well-being (Haar et al., 2014; Ilies et al., 2015), and job performance (Keene & Reynolds, 2005; Wayne et al., 2017). As organizations continue to evolve, understanding the factors that contribute to all types of work-to-family and family-to-work spillover becomes paramount, not just for the well-being of employees but also for organizational health and productivity.

The work-family literature underscores the important role that attention, decision-making, and planning play when workers strategize on how to maintain balance between their work and non-work responsibilities, thereby managing spillover effects between domains (Powell & Greenhaus, 2006; Radcliffe & Cassell, 2014). For example, individuals must make decisions on whether to fully integrate their work and non-work lives (e.g., taking work calls during family dinners or grocery planning during work hours) or attempt to keep them separate (e.g., maintaining strict boundaries between work hours and personal time, or using separate phones for work and personal communication), with different outcomes potentially resulting from the type of strategy workers implement (Kossek & Lautsch, 2008; Michel & Hargis, 2008). Regardless of the strategies an individual uses, achieving “satisfaction and good functioning at work and at home, with a minimum of role conflict” (Clark, 2000, p. 751) puts a large and “enduring” mental toll on workers (Dean et al., 2022, p. 13).

The core cognitive functions responsible for “taking the time to think before acting; meeting novel, unanticipated challenges; resisting temptations; and staying focused” (Diamond, 2013, p. 135) when having to simultaneously attend to work and non-work responsibilities are referred to as executive functions (EFs). Executive functions are a collection of higher order cognitive processes that facilitate managing competing demands and navigating the complexities of everyday life, such as preventing work responsibilities from negatively affecting one’s family life or vice versa. While the term “executive functions” refers to several different interconnected cognitive processes, most models of EF include inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility and are responsible for impulse control, updating and manipulating information, and shifting between tasks (Friedman & Miyake, 2017). EFs exist on a continuum and differ between individuals. Having low levels of executive functioning, such that an individual's day-to-day life is impaired, is referred to as executive dysfunction (Rabinovici et al., 2015). Executive dysfunction can result in an individual struggling with planning and organizing tasks, regulating emotions and behavior, or maintaining attention and focus. Executive dysfunction is a common symptom and byproduct of numerous disorders and disabilities (Diamond, 2013). For example, Individuals with neurodevelopmental disabilities such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) commonly encounter challenges because of impaired executive functions (Alderson et al., 2013). Additionally, mental health disorders, such as major depressive disorder (MDD) or obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), can impair one’s executive functions (Snyder et al., 2015).

When an individual suffers impaired or disabled executive functions, their ability to accomplish work (Barkley & Fischer, 2011; Best et al., 2009; Miller et al., 2012b) or non-work tasks (Davies et al., 2024; Giola et al., 2001; Grahek et al., 2019) in a timely and efficient manner is compromised, thus creating the potential for negative spillover to occur while simultaneously impeding positive spillover between domains. Individuals with executive dysfunction may struggle with planning, time management, and being distracted, making it difficult for them to disconnect from or engage in work or family responsibilities at appropriate times. For example, an individual with executive dysfunction may continue to answer work emails or ruminate about work tasks during family dinners or events, diminishing their presence and participation in family activities (Barkley et al., 2008). Conversely, this same individual may struggle to focus on work responsibilities due to preoccupation with family concerns, such as planning a family member's birthday party or worrying about their child's academic performance, leading to reduced productivity and potential conflicts with colleagues or supervisors. Additionally, their executive dysfunction may inhibit positive spillover, preventing them from applying strategies learned in a professional context to manage their household more effectively (e.g., carrying the confidence gained from successfully leading a work project to implement more effective parenting strategies at home).[MR1] 

Cognitive disabilities that can negatively affect executive functioning are the most common type of disability among working adults, affecting a significant portion of the U.S. workforce. Indeed, the Center for Disease Control (CDC) estimates that 7% of all working adults in the United States between 18 and 65 years old have some form of cognitive impairment that includes executive dysfunction (Shocky et al., 2023)[1]. Although effective boundary management between work and family domains requires substantial executive functioning capabilities, there remains a disconnect between work-family and executive function literatures, with minimal integration of these research streams. Despite the increased call for more research on neurodiverse populations within the workforce (LeFevre -Levy et al., 2023), the cognitive load required to fulfill one’s personal and professional duties (Wayne et al., 2023), and the prevalence of cognitive impairments that interfere with executive functions, empirical examinations of how executive functioning relates to the joint maintenance of work and family responsibilities remain scarce. This gap provides little guidance or best practices for workers, researchers, practitioners, and policy makers when designing work environments that minimize the detrimental effects executive dysfunction may have juggling work and non-work responsibilities, both in terms of preventing negative spillover between domains and facilitating positive spillover that could enhance performance and well-being in both spheres. In addition to the practical implications of this research, this area of inquiry will expand our theoretical understanding of the experiences neuroatypical employees face both inside and outside of their workplace.

This study addresses this critical gap by pursuing three key aims. First, I examine how executive functioning influences both negative and positive spillover between work and family domains. While prior research has established various boundary management strategies for balancing work and family responsibilities (Kossek & Lautsch, 2012; Allen et al., 2014), this study investigates how variations in executive functioning shape individuals' capacity to prevent negative spillover and facilitate positive spillover. This is important because executive dysfunction affects 7% of working adults (Shockey et al., 2023), yet we lack understanding of how these cognitive differences impact their ability to successfully manage work-family boundaries. Without this knowledge, organizations cannot effectively support a significant portion of their workforce who may struggle with boundary management due to cognitive challenges rather than motivational or situational factors[MR2] . Second, I explore the contextual factors, specifically gender and various forms of social support, that moderate the relationship between executive functioning and work-family spillover. By examining these boundary conditions, I provide a more nuanced understanding of when and for whom executive functioning most strongly influences work-family spillover outcomes. Third, I broaden the theoretical scope of work-family research by adding perspectives from cognitive psychology and sociology to the traditionally organizational psychology-dominated field. This interdisciplinary approach illuminates the experiences of neurodiverse employees who have historically been underrepresented in organizational research, while also incorporating sociological insights on how gender roles and expectations shape work-family experiences differently for men and women (Hochschild & Machung, 2012; Daminger, 2019). By examining cognitive differences alongside gendered social dynamics that affect a significant portion of the workforce, this study addresses important gaps in our understanding of workplace inclusion and accommodation.

These aims collectively contribute to both theory and practice. Theoretically, this work expands boundary theory by incorporating executive functioning as a critical individual difference variable, offering a more complete framework for understanding work-family dynamics. Practically, the findings will provide organizations and policymakers with evidence-based guidance for supporting employees with executive dysfunction, potentially improving both employee well-being and organizational outcomes. In the sections that follow, I propose an empirical examination of how executive functioning influences work-family spillover using data from the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS) study. This research will integrate cognitive functioning and work-family literatures, testing hypotheses across two nationally representative samples to understand how executive functioning capabilities shape spillover experiences in the context of gender and social support.

Theoretical Background

Executive Functioning

Executive functions (EF) refer to a set of interrelated, yet distinct, higher-order neurocognitive processes that are vital for consciously regulating thoughts, emotions, and goal-directed behavior (Diamond, 2013; Miyake & Friedman, 2012; Snyder et al., 2015). The conceptualization and operationalization of executive functions have been subjects of ongoing debate and research in clinical psychology, cognitive psychology, and neuroscience. Early models of EF posited a single "central executive" responsible for coordinating and delegating various cognitive tasks (Baddeley, 1996). However, more complex models of EF have emerged in recent years. One widely accepted and empirically supported framework is the unity/diversity model proposed by Miyake and Friedman (2012, 2017). This model posits three core executive functions: working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control (Diamond, 2013; Miyake et al., 2000). These functions display "unity" in that they are highly correlated with each other, but "diversity" in that they also show distinct patterns of association with other outcomes and behaviors. Current approaches to modeling EF frequently leverage a bi-factor approach (e.g., Karr et al., 2018), such that an individual's score on an EF task is conceptualized as being jointly caused by a "General EF" dimension, "specific EF" dimensions, non-EF processes, and measurement error (referred to as the “task impurity problem”, see Snyder et al., 2015).

Despite the proliferation of EF models, working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control are three specific EFs present in most frameworks. Working memory involves temporarily holding and manipulating information in an accessible state (Baddeley, 1992; Unsworth & Engle, 2007), allowing individuals to maintain representations of goals, instructions, and potential consequences over time. This capacity is essential for bridging temporal gaps between perceiving information, planning, and acting (D'Esposito & Postle, 2015; Smith & Jonides, 1999). Cognitive flexibility enables switching between different tasks, rules, or mental perspectives (Miyake et al., 2000; Diamond, 2013), helping us adjust behavior in response to changing priorities or situational demands (Ionescu, 2012). Inhibitory control allows us to override automatic responses to execute more appropriate behaviors (Diamond, 2013; Friedman & Miyake, 2004), including resisting distractions and suppressing irrelevant information.

Hofmann et al. (2012) integrated these three components of working memory maintains goals and plans, inhibitory control shields individuals from counterproductive impulses, and cognitive flexibility facilitates adaptive shifting between priorities into a comprehensive model of self-regulation. Research supports this integration, showing that these three executive functions individually contribute to different aspects of self-regulation. For example, studies have found that working memory capacity predicts one's ability to modify situations to avoid temptation, inhibitory control enhances the ability to direct attention away from distractions, and cognitive flexibility supports adapting strategies when faced with changing circumstances or obstacles (Hofmann et al., 2008; McRae et al., 2012; Hendricks & Buchanan, 2016; Garrison & Schmeichel, 2022; Dajani & Uddin, 2015).

This integrated supervisory system enhances broader capacities for attention, reasoning, decision-making, and problem-solving when pursuing goals. Evidence from multiple research domains demonstrates that executive functioning is associated with enhanced self-regulatory capabilities across numerous contexts. For instance, developmental studies show that executive functioning skills in childhood predict greater delay of gratification and impulse control in later life (Moffitt et al., 2011; Shoda et al., 1990), while research on emotion regulation links higher executive functioning to more effective management of negative emotional reactions through strategic cognitive reappraisal (Bridgett et al., 2015).

Prior empirical research has demonstrated that executive functioning uniquely relates to self-regulation and goal attainment in different settings over the life course. For example, Eisenberg et al. (2004) find that preschoolers with better executive functioning skills exhibited greater self-regulation and goal-directed behavior when faced with conflicting desires, such as choosing between completing an academic task or engaging in a more enjoyable activity. Further, research on academic achievement in adolescence has consistently demonstrated that executive functions like working memory and cognitive flexibility are strong predictors of performance across content areas, over and above the effects of general intelligence (Brydges et al., 2012; Lawson et al., 2017; Spiegel et al., 2022; St Clair-Thompson & Gathercole, 2006).

In adulthood, higher levels of executive functioning predict successfully accomplishing and managing competing or interfering role demands and goals. For example, Allan et al. (2016) found that higher executive functioning was associated with increased adherence to health behaviors such as regular physical exercise and smoking cessation. Additionally, adults with higher levels of executive functioning are more successful at pursuing multiple goal domains simultaneously and weigh “alternative responses and their temporally proximal and distal outcomes” (Antshel et al., 2013, p. 109) without experiencing excessive goal interference. Given that the cognitive control enabled by one’s executive functions is a finite resource with a limited capacity that differs from person to person (Kaplan & Berman, 2010), individuals with lower levels of executive functioning may lack the cognitive resources to attend to multiple goals simultaneously, leading to unaccomplished goals. As Masicampo and Baumeister (2011) and Marien et al. (2012) both demonstrate, impaired executive functions can generate a negative feedback loop, such that limited executive functions lead to unaccomplished tasks, these unaccomplished tasks continue to unconsciously occupy one’s attention in the background, thereby reducing the effort and attention one can give to their current tasks.

The negative outcomes that follow from this failed goal pursuit, among other detrimental outcomes of executive dysfunction, have several implications for people in both work and non-work settings. With respect to workplace and labor market outcomes, Küpper et al. (2012) and Barkley et al. (2010) document how adults with executive dysfunction via attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) experience higher rates of job instability, frequent job changes, unemployment, and underemployment compared to their peers without the disorder. Moreover, individuals with executive dysfunction may have difficulty adapting to structured work environments and may experience interpersonal conflicts with colleagues or supervisors due to impulsivity or inattention. These challenges often result in lower income levels and limited career advancement opportunities for those with executive functioning difficulties (Bailey, 2007; Biederman & Faraone, 2006; Fletcher, 2014).

In non-work settings, executive dysfunction can have a significant impact on marriage and family outcomes. Individuals with executive functioning difficulties may struggle with communication, emotional regulation, and maintaining a consistent level of engagement in their relationships (Orlov, 2010). They may experience difficulties in managing household responsibilities, parenting duties, and financial matters, which can lead to increased stress and conflict within the family (Eakin et al., 2004). Murphy and Barkley (1996) show that couples where one or both partners have ADHD may experience higher rates of marital dissatisfaction, separation, and divorce compared to couples without the disorder. Lastly, individuals with lower levels of executive functioning have partners who report experiencing a lower quality of life (Dawson et al. 2015).

These findings converge on the fact that executive functions underlie the self-regulation and management of competing goals and role responsibilities in both work (Chan et al., 2021) and non-work (Zelazo et al., 2024) settings. Individuals with higher levels of EF have the cognitive capacities to juggle multiple pursuits by maintaining focal priorities, ignoring distractions, adjusting their strategies, and avoiding counterproductive impulses that could disrupt goal attainment (Strobach et al., 2018). Individuals with lower levels of EF may struggle to effectively manage multiple priorities and goals across work and personal life domains and are more likely to experience difficulties with attention regulation, goal management, and impulse control (Adler & Shaw, 2011). Consequently, they may experience greater difficulty in successfully navigating the demands of work and -life balance, becoming more prone to role conflicts, reduced goal attainment, and increased stress levels as they struggle to effectively manage competing responsibilities across work and non-work domains (Orlov, 2010). The inability to manage the responsibilities within each domain may establish conditions for negative spillover to occur from work to family life or vice versa and interfere with positive spillover from taking place. In the next section, I provide an overview of spillover, both positive and negative, highlighting the role executive functioning may play with respect to spillover.

Work Family Spillover

Work-family spillover occurs when experiences, attitudes, emotions, skills, behaviors, or psychological states are transferred from one domain to another in a way that impacts functioning in the receiving domain (Bhowon, 2013; Grzywacz & Marks, 2000; Hanson et al., 2006). This bi-directional transmission of experiences can take two distinct, yet interrelated, forms: positive spillover that enhances functioning in the receiving domain and negative spillover that impairs functioning in the receiving domain.

Negative Spillover. Negative work-to-family spillover and family-to-work spillover (i.e., “conflict”, see Allen et al., 2023 and Grzywacz & Marks, 2000, p. 7) embodies the depleting, interference-based pathway in which strain, stress, and preoccupation generated in one domain impairs one's capacity to fulfill demands in the other domain. For example, the cognitive fatigue and irritability resulting from excessive work hours may spillover to inhibit an employee's patience and engagement with family activities (Rothbard, 2001).

            Several theoretical perspectives on spillover have described the decision-making process individuals engage in when maintaining balance and preventing spillover between domains. These perspectives recognize that individuals are not passive recipients of work and family demands, but active agents who make conscious choices about how to allocate their time, energy, and resources across different life roles. One influential framework in this area is the resource-based perspective proposed by Edwards and Rothbard (2000). This approach suggests that individuals have a finite pool of personal resources such as time, energy, and attention that they must distribute between work and family domains. The allocation of these limited resources involves a series of decisions about how much to invest in each domain, based on factors such as role salience, perceived demands, and anticipated outcomes. Edwards and Rothbard argue that individuals engage in a process of resource allocation that aims to maximize positive outcomes and minimize negative consequences across both domains. Building on this resource-based view, other researchers have explored the cognitive processes involved in work-family decision-making. For example, Greenhaus and Powell (2003) discuss how work-family decision-making and effectively allocating time between domains depends “not only on situational factors (role pressure) but on personal characteristics” (p. 302). Additionally, the boundary management perspective (Ashforth et al., 2000) highlights how individuals make decisions about segmenting or integrating their work and family roles. This approach recognizes that people differ in their preferences for keeping work and family separate or allowing them to blend, and that these preferences inform the strategies they use to manage the boundaries between domains.

Another perspective on decision making and work-family spillover is the selection, optimization, and compensation (SOC) model proposed by Baltes and colleagues (Baltes, 1997; Baltes & Baltes, 1990). The SOC model posits that successful adaptation to changing ecological contexts across the lifespan requires employing strategies of selection (i.e., identifying and prioritizing goals), optimization (i.e., acquiring and refining goal-relevant means), and compensation (i.e., invoking alternative means to maintain functioning in the face of losses). Applied to the work-family interface, the model suggests that individuals must continually select valued goals across both domains, optimize investment of finite personal resources to achieve those aims, and compensate for losses or constraints in one domain by reallocating effort or generating new resources from the other. For instance, an employee might scale back involvement in non-essential work projects to conserve time for family responsibilities (selection), hone time management skills to juggle roles efficiently (optimization) and draw on emotional support from family to replenish resources depleted by workplace demands (compensation). The SOC framework underscores the significant cognitive effort required as individuals engage in the ongoing processes of selecting priorities, optimizing resource allocation, and compensating for constraints across work and family domains.

Building on this perspective, action regulation theory offers another lens through which to understand work-family decision making. While the original theory, developed by Frese and Zapf (1994) and elaborated by Zacher and Frese (2018), explains how individuals generally regulate behavior through cognitive processes like goal development, orientation, planning, monitoring, and feedback processing, Hirschi et al., 2019 developed a distinct model applying these principles specifically to work-family balance. Hirschi et al.’s model conceptualizes balance as the successful joint pursuit of work and family goals through four specific action strategies: allocating resources, changing resources and barriers, sequencing goals, and revising goals. Unlike previous models that focus primarily on the role of demands and resources, Hirschi et al.'s approach emphasizes the individual's agency in shaping their environment and development while uniquely addressing how people manage multiple goals across work and family domains. This model considers how people adaptively use different strategies based on the availability and malleability of resources, the presence of barriers, and the time constraints associated with different goals (Vancouver, Weinhardt, & Schmidt, 2010). This dynamic approach accounts for the complex interplay between work and family domains, acknowledging that strategies may need to be adjusted over time as circumstances change. By focusing on the active pursuit and attainment of work and family goals, this theoretical framework provides insight into how individuals can achieve balance through the flexible use of various goal-oriented strategies. Moreover, it extends the understanding of multiple goal pursuit in the context of work and family roles, addressing a gap in previous research that often focused on competing goals within a single domain (Unsworth et al., 2014).

The decision-making processes these different theories describe demonstrate how cognitively demanding maintaining work and non-work responsibilities can be. For individuals with impaired executive functioning, the challenges of managing work-family spillover may be particularly pronounced. Deficits in executive functioning can limit the cognitive (e.g., difficulty focusing during work conversations) and emotional (e.g., snapping at family after work, see Holley et al., 2017) resources available to an individual, making it harder to effectively allocate these resources between work and family domains and implement strategies these theories describe. Executive functioning deficits impair the brain's ability to regulate attention and emotions, leading to difficulties managing the mental and emotional demands of balancing work and family roles. This can lead to a range of negative consequences for both the individual, their family, and their workplace, including increased stress, reduced performance in both domains, and diminished overall well-being.

To my knowledge, no studies to date have directly examined the relationship between executive functioning and work-family outcomes, though research on adjacent constructs suggests this is an important area for investigation (e.g., ADHD and labor market outcomes: Fletcher, 2014; Küpper et al., 2012; ADHD and family conflict: Eakin et al., 2004; Johnston & Mash, 2001). Lower levels of executive function (i.e., executive dysfunction) may result in negative work-family outcomes (i.e., negative work-to-family and family-to-work spillover) due to difficulties in managing time effectively within a given domain. Positive work-family spillover (i.e., “enhancement”; see Grzywacz & Marks, 2000, p. 7) represents a positive, enhancing pathway whereby involvement in one role provides resources (e.g., skills, perspectives, positive mood, confidence) that improve performance or affective experiences in the other role (Greenhaus & Powell, 2006; Wayne et al., 2022). Additionally, positive spillover can go from the family domain to the work domain and vice versa (Grzywacz & Carlson, 2007). For example, positive work-to-family spillover may occur when an employee who gains leadership and interpersonal abilities from their workplace responsibilities leverages those competencies to become a more effective parent. Further, positive family-to-work spillover may occur if the time an employee spends with their family recharges and energizes them for the following workday.

For example, the cognitive flexibility inherent in executive functioning could enable an employee to successfully shift mindsets between analytical thinking required at work and the different attentional demands of parenting at home (Ferguson et al., 2015), inhibitory control aspects of executive functioning may help employees override tendencies toward negative work rumination and instead focus fully on family activities. [MR3] Additionally, higher executive functioning may buffer against negative work-family spillover by enhancing employees' self-regulatory capacity to prevent strain, depletion, or interference from crossing role boundaries[MR4] . Individuals with greater working memory abilities may be better able to actively maintain their priorities, schedules, and work-family plans, reducing inter-role conflict[MR5]  through more effective planning and time management. Overall, higher levels of executive functioning have the potential to enhance individuals' capacity to navigate work-family role boundaries and promote harmonious, enriching integration while minimizing depletion (Lapierre et al., 2018). Moderating Relationships

Gender. Given the different societal expectations men and women face when it comes to dealing with the boundaries between work and non-work, there is reason to believe that gender may moderate the relationship between executive functioning and spillover, primarily through the increased household management demands expected of women. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that there are no gender differences in executive functioning (Grissom & Reyes, 2019) or work-family conflict (Shockley et al., 2017). However, traditional gender roles and societal expectations often place a greater burden on women to manage household and caregiving responsibilities, even when they are employed full-time (Hochschild & Machung, 2012). This unequal distribution of domestic labor can lead to increased strain on women's cognitive resources, making them more vulnerable to the negative effects of executive dysfunction on work-family spillover (Ciciolla & Luthar, 2019).

Women frequently report spending more time on "invisible labor" (e.g., household managerial tasks and childcare, see Lareau & Weininger, 2008) compared to men, regardless of their employment status (Bianchi et al., 2012; Offer & Schneider, 2011). This "second shift" of domestic work can be mentally and physically taxing, leaving women with less time and energy to devote to their professional responsibilities. Moreover, women more often take on the cognitive dimension of household labor, which involves the mental effort required to plan, organize, and manage household tasks (Daminger, 2019; Offer & Schneider, 2011). The cognitive load associated with managing both work and family responsibilities can lead to increased stress, fatigue, and burnout among women (Eby et al., 2005; Nadkarni & Biswas, 2022), negatively impacting their ability to focus, prioritize tasks, and make decisions effectively in both their professional and personal lives. Further, constantly switching between work and family roles can lead to cognitive interference, making it more challenging for women to fully engage in either domain (Allen et al., 2015; Kossek & Lautsch, 2012).[MR7] 

Research by Daminger (2019), Dean et al. (2022), and Wayne et al. (2023) demonstrates  that women bear a disproportionate share of cognitive labor and mental load in household management and the byproducts of such labor are less tangible than their male partners. Their findings show that women take on more anticipation and monitoring tasks at home, creating an additional cognitive burden beyond physical domestic duties. Given that executive functioning is responsible for cognitive control processes like attention, working memory, and task-switching, the increased cognitive demands that women face are likely to tax these functions more heavily. Based on these findings, I propose that this increased cognitive load likely strains women's executive functioning capacities, making it more difficult to maintain boundaries between work and family roles compared to men.[MR8] 

Gender [MR9] role expectations and socialization also create different contexts within which executive functioning operates for men and women managing work-family boundaries. Women are often socialized to prioritize family responsibilities and may experience greater guilt or self-blame when their work interferes with family life (Glavin et al., 2011). This internalization of traditional gender roles can strengthen the relationship between executive functioning and negative work-family spillover by increasing the cognitive effort women expend trying to meet heightened expectations in both domains. In contrast, men may be less likely to experience such guilt due to societal expectations that prioritize their role as breadwinners (Offer, 2014), potentially weakening the relationship between executive functioning deficits and work-family spillover as they face less pressure to maintain equal engagement across domains.

The cognitive demands on women likely magnify the consequences of executive functioning challenges, particularly for negative spillover in both directions. Women with lower executive functioning[MR10]  may face compounded difficulties when managing the numerous transitions between work and family domains, as they must navigate more complex and frequent role transitions throughout the day. The added cognitive burden women face from household management may deplete the executive resources needed to prevent negative spillover, both from work to family and from family to work. For negative family-to-work spillover, women's greater responsibility for anticipating and addressing family needs may make it particularly difficult to compartmentalize family concerns during work hours when executive functioning is compromised. Similarly, for negative work-to-family spillover, women with lower executive functioning may struggle more than men to disengage from work thoughts and responsibilities during family time, as they must simultaneously manage their greater share of household cognitive labor. The same gendered dynamics that may create additional burdens for women could theoretically affect positive spillover in multiple ways. On the one hand, women's greater involvement in managing both work and family domains could provide more opportunities for skills, perspectives, and positive experiences to transfer between roles (Offer & Schneider, 2011; Powell & Greenhaus, 2010). Their heightened engagement across domains might allow women with stronger executive functioning to more readily identify and capitalize on instances where resources from one domain could benefit the other[MR11] . Conversely, the increased cognitive load women face might make it more challenging to recognize and leverage positive spillover opportunities, even with robust executive functioning capabilities. For men, traditional gender roles that compartmentalize work and family responsibilities could either limit positive spillover by reducing cross-domain engagement or enhance it by allowing clearer recognition of transferable benefits when they do occur [MR12] (Rothbard & Edwards, 2003; Swanberg, 2004). The complexity of these potential mechanisms, combined with the lack of clear empirical evidence for gender differences in positive spillover, makes this an important area for exploration. Understanding how gender moderates the relationship between executive functioning and positive spillover could reveal unexplored pathways for promoting beneficial work-family interactions across gender identities. Social Support. Studies examining the work-family dynamics interface have established that access to resources in both work and family domains significantly shapes how individuals experience work-family conflict (Allen et al., 2000), spillover (Grzywacz & Marks, 2000), and enrichment (Greenhaus & Powell, 2006), ultimately affecting both personal and organizational outcomes. Specifically, the availability of support in either domain plays a critical role in determining how effectively individuals navigate work-family dynamicsdemands. This perspective is grounded in several theories, such as conservation of resources theory (COR; Hobfoll, 1989) which posits that individuals strive to obtain, retain, protect, and foster resources that they value, and stress occurs when there is a threat of resource loss, an actual loss, or a lack of expected gain in resources following resource investment. Additionally, the job demands-resources model (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007) points out that job resources, such as social support, can buffer the impact of job demands on strain, including work-family conflict. Parallel to the work-family literature, the availability of support as a resource has been frequently shown to benefit individuals with cognitive deficits and difficulties with executive functioning (Diamond, 2013b; Tun et al., 2013; Sims et al., 2011). For example, Seeman et al. (2001) find that social support can improve cognitive functioning and well-being in older adults with executive function impairments. Further, research shows that supportive environments can help children with ADHD, who often struggle with executive functioning, to better manage their symptoms and improve academic and social outcomes (Langberg et al., 2011). [MR13] Taken together, these findings suggest that support may be particularly important for individuals with lower levels of executive functioning in managing spillover between work and non-work domains, making it crucial to understand how different forms of support manifest across various life contexts.

Within the organizational domain, support can take multiple forms, from formal policies to day-to-day interpersonal interactions. Informal support within organizations primarily comes from supervisors and coworkers, with supervisors often having greater ability to implement policies and provide resources that directly affect an employee's ability to manage work and family demands (Thompson & Prottas, 2006). Empirical evidence from Clark et al. (2017) highlights the importance of a supportive environment in mitigating the negative effects of work and family stressors on work-family outcomes, finding "informal support factors were generally more impactful than formal organizational support factors such as flextime and telecommuting" (p. 1540). Drawing from boundary theory (Ashforth et al., 2000), which explains how individuals create, maintain, and cross the psychological boundaries between different life domains such as work and family, a supportive environment can provide individuals with additional resources to manage the demands of work and family roles effectively. Boundary theory suggests that individuals vary in how they prefer to segment or integrate their work and family roles, and that environmental support plays a crucial role in helping individuals maintain their preferred boundary management strategies.

When organizations and families provide support that aligns with an individual's boundary management preferences and capabilities, they can better navigate the transitions between work and family domains and more effectively manage their responsibilities in each role[MR14] . These environments may help compensate for the deficits caused by EF limitations (Williams et al., 2009). For example, supportive supervisors and coworkers might provide additional structure and clear deadlines for employees who have difficulty with planning and organization. Similarly, family support systems might include partners who help create and maintain routines, or who assist with breaking down complex household tasks into manageable steps for those who struggle with executive planning. Various forms of informal support have the potential to buffer against the negative effects of EF deficits on work-family spillover, reducing instances of negative spillover such as work-related distractions during family time or family-related stress affecting work performance (Allen et al., 2015; Carlson & Perrewé, 1999; Ferguson et al., 2016).

Supervisor support is defined as the extent to which supervisors "provide encouragement and support to employees within their work groups" (Griffin et al., 2001, p. 537). It includes emotional support, instrumental support, role modeling, and creative work-family management (Hammer et al., 2009). Supervisor support, particularly family-supportive supervisor behaviors (FSSB), has been consistently linked to reduced work-family conflict and increased work-family enrichment (Kossek et al., 2011). For individuals with executive dysfunction, supervisor support can play a crucial role in workplace success. Supervisors who understand the nature of executive function challenges can provide accommodations and strategies that significantly improve job performance and satisfaction (Adamou et al., 2013). For instance, supervisors can offer clear, structured guidelines and frequent feedback, which can help compensate for difficulties in self-monitoring and task initiation often experienced by those with executive dysfunction (Barkley, 2012). [MR15] Drawing from the job demands-resources model (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007), supervisor support can be conceptualized as a job resource that can buffer the impact of executive function deficits on work-family spillover. This support may help mitigate negative spillover by providing structure, guidance, and accommodation that helps employees better manage their work responsibilities, potentially preventing work-related stress from affecting family life. [MR16] Thus, I hypothesize the following:

H7: Supervisor support will moderate the relationship between executive functioning and negative family-to-work spillover, such that the negative relationship between executive functioning and negative family-to-work spillover will be weaker (i.e. less negative) as levels of supervisor support increase.

H8: Supervisor support will moderate the relationship between executive functioning and negative work-to-family spillover, such that the negative relationship between executive functioning and negative work-to-family spillover will be weaker (i.e. less negative) as levels of supervisor support increase.

While the buffering effects of supervisor support on negative spillover are well-documented, the role of supervisor support in facilitating positive spillover for individuals with varying levels of executive function is less clear. For positive spillover, supportive supervision may enhance the transfer of positive experiences and resources between domains through multiple mechanisms. For instance, supportive supervision could help individuals with lower EF better recognize and leverage their unique strengths, potentially leading to increased confidence that transfers between domains. Additionally, when supervisors provide structured guidance and positive feedback, this may help employees develop compensatory strategies that could benefit both work and family roles. However, the exact nature of these relationships remains unclear, particularly given the complex interplay between executive function and resource utilization. Therefore, I propose the following research questions regarding supervisor support, executive functioning, and positive spillover:

It encompasses multiple dimensions, including instrumental (e.g., helping with a work task) and affective (e.g., listening to a colleague's concerns) support (Chiaburu & Harrison, 2008). For individuals with executive dysfunction, coworker support can be particularly beneficial in managing workplace challenges. Coworkers can provide instrumental support by helping to break down complex tasks, offering reminders for deadlines, and assisting with prioritization (Biederman et al., 2006). Within the family domain, social support primarily comes from spouses (Michel et al., 2011). Spousal support plays a crucial role in managing work-family demands (King et al., 1995) and has been identified as critical for marital satisfaction and reducing family-to-work conflict (Michel et al., 2011). For individuals with executive dysfunction, spousal support can be invaluable in managing both work and home life. The challenges associated with executive dysfunction often extend beyond the workplace, affecting home organization, time management, and relationship dynamics (Barkley et al., 2008). Methods

Sample and Procedures

This study will use data from the second and third waves of the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS) study, collected between 2004-2006 and 2012-2014, respectively. The MIDUS study is a national longitudinal research project focused on understanding factors that influence health and well-being during midlife and beyond. All analyses will be conducted on both samples to ensure the robustness of this study's findings.

The MIDUS study’s sampling design employed a stratified, random-digit-dial (RDD) sampling frame to obtain a nationally representative sample of non-institutionalized, English-speaking adults from the 48 contiguous United States. The sampling procedure used a multistage probability design with stratification by geographic region and metropolitan statistical areas (MSA) [MR18] to ensure adequate representation across different demographic segments. The original MIDUS study (Wave 1: 1995-1996) established the baseline cohort, with subsequent waves following up with these participants over time.

The second wave of the MIDUS survey (MIDUS II) included n = 4,963 non-institutionalized adults aged 32-84, representing a 75% retention rate from the original sample. MIDUS II data collection involved a comprehensive, multimodal assessment protocol that included telephone interviews (approximately 30 minutes) followed by self-administered questionnaires (SAQs) mailed to participants (taking approximately 2 hours to complete). Wave 3 (MIDUS III) included n = 3,294 non-institutionalized adults from the Wave 2 sample, representing a 66% retention rate between survey waves.

For the present investigation, final analyses will be performed on a subsample of MIDUS participants who 1) are in dual-earner[MR19]  couples, 2) employed at the time of data collection, and 3) have complete data on all variables of interest. This selection ensures that participants have the potential to experience both work and family role demands, which is essential for examining work-family spillover phenomena.

Measures[MR20] 

Executive Functioning. Executive functioning will be assessed using a subset of tests from the Brief Test of Adult Cognition by Telephone (BTACT; Lachman et al., 2014). The BTACT is a comprehensive cognitive battery designed to be administered over the telephone in approximately 20 minutes. The executive functioning factor of the BTACT consists of five tests out of the seven contained in the BTACT:[MR21] 

  1. The Backward Digit Span measures working memory by having participants repeat increasingly long strings of digits in reverse order (Wechsler, 1997).
  2. Category Fluency measures verbal fluency and executive function by having participants generate as many words as possible from a given category (e.g., animals) in 1 minute (Borkowski et al., 1967; Tombaugh et al., 1999).
  3. Number Series measures inductive reasoning by having participants determine the next number in a series based on a pattern (Salthouse & Prill, 1987).
  4. The 30-Seconds and Counting Task (30-SACT) measures processing speed by having participants count backwards from 100 as quickly as possible for 30 seconds (Tun & Lachman, 2008).
  5. The Stop and Go Switch Task (SGST) measures task-switching ability and reaction time in an executive function task involving switching between rules for verbal responses (Tun & Lachman, 2008).

In the present study, executive functioning will be operationalized using a composite score derived from multiple BTACT exercises[MR22] : the mean of z-scores for Digits Backward, Category Fluency, Number Series, Backward Counting, and the mean of switch and nonswitch trials in the Stop & Go Switch Task. This composite score is computed following the scoring procedure outlined by the Tun and Lachman (2012). Both the individual exercise responses as well as the pre-computed composite score are available in the MIDUS database. Thus, the executive functioning composite scores to be used in this analysis will be those calculated by the MIDUS research team following their established protocol rather than computed independently for this study. This approach of using composite scores helps address the concerns regarding the task impurity problem mentioned earlier, as combining multiple measures into latent variables or factor scores has been shown to considerably improve measurement reliability and validity compared to using individual tasks or difference scores alone (Enkavi et al., 2019). The psychometric structure of the BTACT has previously been validated in prior studies (Gurnani et al., 2015; Tun et al., 2006) and, although the MIDUS documentation does not report reliability statistics for the BTACT[MR23] , test-retest reliability coefficients from prior studies computing the executive functioning factor ranged from .80 to .88 over 1 to 4 weeks (see Table 7 of Lachman et al., 2014), indicating strong temporal stability of the measure.

Spillover. Positive and negative family-to-work and work-to-family spillover will be measured on a five-point frequency scale ranging from 1 (“All the time”) to 5 (“Never”) using the scale developed by Grzywacz and colleagues (Grzywacz, 2000; Grzywacz & Marks, 2000a; Grzywacz & Marks, 2000b; Grzywacz & Marks, 2001) that is included in all panels of the MIDUS study. Sample items for negative work-to-family spillover include: “Stress at work makes you irritable at home” and “Job worries or problems distract you when you are at home”. Sample items for negative family-to-work spillover include: “Responsibilities at home reduce the effort you can devote to your job” and “Stress at home makes you irritable at work”. Sample items for positive work-to-family spillover include: “The things you do at work help you deal with personal and practical issues at home.” and “The skills you use on your job are useful for things you have to do at home.”. Sample items for positive family-to-work spillover include: “Providing for what is needed at home makes you work harder at your job.” and “Your home life helps you relax and feel ready for the next day’s work.”.  Reliability values for the four scales in the full sample ranged from 0.71 to 0.82. This work-family spillover scale has been used extensively in prior empirical work and has been validated against common measures of WFC (e.g., Netemeyer et al., 1996, see Table A1 of Allen et al., 2023 for more information).

Coworker support. Coworker support will be measured using items originally developed for the Whitehall Health Survey of British Civil Servants (Marmot et al., 1991), which were later incorporated as a subscale within the larger job characteristics scale within MIDUS. Participants are asked to rate the extent to which they agree with the given statements using five-point frequency scale, ranging from 1 (“All the time”) to 5 (“Never”). The items include “How often do you get help and support from your coworkers” and “How often are your coworkers willing to listen to your work-related problems.” Scores will be reverse-coded, such that higher scores will reflect higher amounts of support. Reliability values for coworker support were 0.67 in the second wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample and 0.95 in the third wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample.

Supervisor support. Supervisor support will be measured using items originally developed for the Whitehall Health Survey of British Civil Servants (Marmot et al., 1991), which were later incorporated as a subscale within the larger job characteristics scale in MIDUS. Participants are asked to rate the extent to which they agree with the given statements using five-point frequency scale, ranging from 1 (“All the time”) to 5 (“Never”). The items include “How often do you get the information you need from your supervisor or superiors,” “How often do you get help and support from your immediate supervisor,” and “How often is your immediate supervisor willing to listen to your work-related problems.”. Scores will be reverse-coded, such that higher scores will reflect higher amounts of support. Reliability values for coworker support were 0.87 in the second wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample and 0.97 in the third wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample.

Spousal Support. Spousal support will be measured using the Social Support and Strain Scale, which assesses the perception of caring and understanding an individual receives from their family, friends, and partners (Walen & Lachman, 2000). This measure has been used extensively in prior empirical studies examining spousal support with the MIDUS sample (Donoho et al., 2015; Grzywacz & Marks, 2000, 2001; Jiao & Grzywavz, 2023; Ulichney et al., 2023) Items include: “How much does your spouse or partner really care about you?”, “How much does he or she understand the way you feel about things?”, “How much does he or she appreciate you?”, “How much do you rely on him or her for help if you have a serious problem?”, “How much can you open up to him or her if you need to talk about your worries?”, and “How much can you relax and be yourself around him or her?” Each item was scored on a 1 (“A lot.”) to 4 (“Not at all.”). Scores will be constructed by calculating the mean of the values of the items in each scale. Items will be reverse-coded so that high scores reflect higher levels of received spousal support. Reliability values for spousal support were 0.90 in the second wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample and 0.91 in the third wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample.

Control Variables

Job Demands. [MR24] Job demands will be included following the approach used by Allen et al. (2023) who use the job demands subscale from the larger job characteristics scale included in MIDUS. Participants are asked to rate the extent to which they agree with the given statements using five-point frequency scale, ranging from 1 (“All the time”) to 5 (“Never”). Job demands items measure the extent to which a participant finds his or her job responsibilities to be demanding. Items include how often: “Do you have to work very intensively – that is, you are very busy trying to get things done?”; “Do different people or groups at work demand things from you that you think are hard to combine?”; “(How often) do you have too many demands made on you.”; “(How often) you have enough time to get everything done?”; “(How often) you have a lot of interruption?” The composite score will be constructed by calculating the sum of the reverse-coded values of the items in the scale. Reliability values for spousal support were 0.73 in the second wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample and 0.76 in the third wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample.

Family Demands. Family demands will be computed using the strategy by Allen et al., (2023) who combined three items measuring time/stress demands at home: (“You have too many demands made on you.”, “You have enough time to get everything done.”, “You have a lot of interruptions.”) with two items measuring family support (“Not including your spouse or partner, how often do members of your family make too many demands on you?”,  “How often does your spouse or partner make too many demands on you?”). The three items measuring Time/Stress Demands at Home are based on a five-point scale that ranged from “All the time” (1) to “Never” (5). The two-items measuring family support are based on a four-point scale that ranged from “Often” (1) to “Never” (4). Given the different number of scale points across the items, items will be transformed using the percent of maximum possible (POMP) method (Cohen et al., 1999): POMP = 100 × [(Observed score − Minimum possible)⁄(Maximum possible − Minimum possible)]. Using this method, the minimum and maximum scores across items will be equivalent (i.e., 0 and 100, respectively). Additionally, responses will be reverse-scored when appropriate, such that higher scores will indicate greater family demands.

In addition to family and job demands, household-level variables that will be controlled for in all analyses include household income and household size. Given the affordances income can have on against executive functioning deficits (Duncan et al., 2017) as well as the increased demands a larger household can place on an individual, controlling for these factors will aid in isolating the effect of executive functioning on spillover. Individual-level variables that will be controlled for in all analyses include age, gender, and education level (measured on a 12-point scale from "No school/some grade school" to "Ph.D./Professional degree", capturing key milestones like high school graduation, associate's, bachelor's, and master's degrees).[MR25] [MR26]

Proposed Analysis

A series of generalized estimating equations (GEE) models will be used to test all hypotheses of interest. GEE models are a statistical approach that extends standard regression techniques to handle clustered or correlated data by treating the clustering as a nuisance parameter while still producing valid estimates and standard errors (McNeish, 2023). This analytical approach is appropriate for as GEE models account for the non-independence of observations within households, addressing the violation of independent residuals that occurs because partners' work-family experiences occur within shared household contexts. GEE models handle this by estimating and incorporating the working correlation between observations within clusters (i.e., households) into the estimation process, while maintaining the interpretation of coefficients at the individual level. The method employs robust "sandwich" estimators (Huber, 1967; White, 1980) for computing standard errors that account for potential misspecification in the model's correlation structure by using the empirical correlations in the observed data to adjust the standard errors, thus ensuring valid statistical inference even if the assumed working correlation structure is incorrect.

GEE is particularly well-suited for this study because our primary interest lies in understanding individual-level relationships between executive functioning and work-family spillover, rather than examining couple-level effects or variance decomposition between individual and couple levels[MR27] . As McNeish et al. (2017) argue, GEE provides a more parsimonious approach when cluster-specific inference is not of substantive interest. GEE allows us to obtain population-averaged estimates while appropriately accounting for within-couple correlation, without requiring explicit modeling of random effects or making assumptions about their distributions.
