CHAPTER 1Introduction      The modern workplace has experienced numerous changes in recent years, making the boundaries between one’s work and personal life increasingly blurred. This interplay between work and non-work has been a topic of interest in the organizational sciences given the negative and positive outcomes that can result from employees simultaneously juggling their professional responsibilities and non-work obligations (Allen et al., 2020; Hetrick et al., 2023). Work-to-family and family-to-work spillover are common phenomena that describe how experiences and responsibilities in one domain (e.g., one’s work or family life) both positively and negatively affect the other (Grzywacz & Marks, 2000). Spillover between domains has been shown to predict a wide range of outcomes for both workers and organizations, such as job satisfaction (Lourel et al., 2009), marital quality (Sandberg et al., 2013), employee well-being (Haar et al., 2014; Ilies et al., 2015), and job performance (Keene & Reynolds, 2005; Wayne et al., 2017). As organizations continue to evolve, understanding the factors that contribute to all types of work-to-family and family-to-work spillover becomes paramount, not just for the well-being of employees but also for organizational health and productivity.      The work-family literature underscores the important role that attention, decision-making, and planning play when workers strategize on how to maintain balance between their work and non-work responsibilities, thereby managing spillover effects between domains (Powell & Greenhaus, 2006; Radcliffe & Cassell, 2014). For example, individuals must make decisions on whether to fully integrate their work and non-work lives (e.g., taking work calls during family dinners or grocery planning during work hours) or attempt to keep them separate (e.g., maintaining strict boundaries between work hours and personal time, or using separate phones for work and personal communication), with different outcomes potentially resulting from the type of strategy workers implement (Kossek & Lautsch, 2008; Michel & Hargis, 2008). Regardless of the strategies an individual uses, achieving “satisfaction and good functioning at work and at home, with a minimum of role conflict” (Clark, 2000, p. 751) puts a large and “enduring” mental toll on workers (Dean et al., 2022, p. 13).      The core cognitive functions responsible for “taking the time to think before acting; meeting novel, unanticipated challenges; resisting temptations; and staying focused” (Diamond, 2013, p. 135) when having to simultaneously attend to work and non-work responsibilities are referred to as executive functions (EFs). Executive functions are a collection of higher order cognitive processes that facilitate managing competing demands and navigating the complexities of everyday life, such as preventing work responsibilities from negatively affecting one’s family life or vice versa. While the term “executive functions” refers to several different interconnected cognitive processes, most models of EF include inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility and are responsible for impulse control, updating and manipulating information, and shifting between tasks (Friedman & Miyake, 2017). EFs exist on a continuum and differ between individuals. Having low levels of executive functioning, such that an individual's day-to-day life is impaired, is referred to as executive dysfunction (Rabinovici et al., 2015). Executive dysfunction can result in an individual struggling with planning and organizing tasks, regulating emotions and behavior, or maintaining attention and focus. Executive dysfunction is a common symptom and byproduct of numerous disorders and disabilities (Diamond, 2013). For example, Individuals with neurodevelopmental disabilities such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) commonly encounter challenges because of impaired executive functions (Alderson et al., 2013). Additionally, mental health disorders, such as major depressive disorder (MDD) or obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), can impair one’s executive functions (Snyder et al., 2015).      When an individual suffers impaired or disabled executive functions, their ability to accomplish work (Barkley & Fischer, 2011; Best et al., 2009; Miller et al., 2012b) or non-work tasks (Davies et al., 2024; Giola et al., 2001; Grahek et al., 2019) in a timely and efficient manner is compromised, thus creating the potential for negative spillover to occur while simultaneously impeding positive spillover between domains. Individuals with executive dysfunction may struggle with planning, time management, and being distracted, making it difficult for them to disconnect from or engage in work or family responsibilities at appropriate times. For example, an individual with executive dysfunction may continue to answer work emails or ruminate about work tasks during family dinners or events, diminishing their presence and participation in family activities (Barkley et al., 2008). Conversely, this same individual may struggle to focus on work responsibilities due to preoccupation with family concerns, such as planning a family member's birthday party or worrying about their child's academic performance, leading to reduced productivity and potential conflicts with colleagues or supervisors. Additionally, their executive dysfunction may inhibit positive spillover, preventing them from applying strategies learned in a professional context to manage their household more effectively. Transferring resources across role boundaries requires cognitive flexibility to recognize that skills or affect from one domain are applicable in another, and working memory to hold and deploy those resources across contexts. For instance, confidence gained from leading a work project may not translate into more effective parenting if one lacks the set-shifting capacity to generalize that resource to a different domain.      Cognitive disabilities that can negatively affect executive functioning are the most common type of disability among working adults, affecting a significant portion of the U.S. workforce. Indeed, the Center for Disease Control (CDC) estimates that 7% of all working adults in the United States between 18 and 65 years old have some form of cognitive impairment that includes executive dysfunction (Shocky et al., 2023)1. Although effective boundary management between work and family domains requires substantial executive functioning capabilities, there remains a disconnect between work-family and executive function literatures, with minimal integration of these research streams. Despite the increased call for more research on neurodiverse populations within the workforce (LeFevre -Levy et al., 2023), the cognitive load required to fulfill one’s personal and professional duties (Wayne et al., 2023), and the prevalence of cognitive impairments that interfere with executive functions, empirical examinations of how executive functioning relates to the joint maintenance of work and family responsibilities remain scarce. This gap provides little guidance or best practices for workers, researchers, practitioners, and policy makers when designing work environments that minimize the detrimental effects executive dysfunction may have juggling work and non-work responsibilities, both in terms of preventing negative spillover between domains and facilitating positive spillover that could enhance performance and well-being in both spheres. In addition to the practical implications of this research, this area of inquiry will expand our theoretical understanding of the experiences neuroatypical employees face both inside and outside of their workplace.      This study addresses this critical gap by pursuing three key aims. First, I examine how executive functioning influences both negative and positive spillover between work and family domains. While prior research has established various boundary management strategies for balancing work and family responsibilities (Kossek & Lautsch, 2012; Allen et al., 2014), this study investigates how variations in executive functioning shape individuals' capacity to prevent negative spillover and facilitate positive spillover. This is important because executive dysfunction affects 7% of working adults (Shockey et al., 2023), yet we lack understanding of how these cognitive differences impact their ability to successfully manage work-family boundaries. Without this knowledge, organizations cannot effectively support a significant portion of their workforce who may struggle with boundary management due to cognitive challenges rather than motivational or situational factors. For example, organizations could encourage practices such as designating end-of-day periods for lower-demand administrative tasks, establishing norms against assigning new work near shift transitions, or providing greater scheduling predictability, each of which reduces the cognitive load required to manage work-family boundaries rather than assuming employees simply need more motivation or flexibility. Second, I explore the contextual factors, specifically gender and various forms of social support, that moderate the relationship between executive functioning and work-family spillover. By examining these boundary conditions, I provide a more nuanced understanding of when and for whom executive functioning most strongly influences work-family spillover outcomes. Third, I broaden the theoretical scope of work-family research by adding perspectives from cognitive psychology and sociology to the traditionally organizational psychology-dominated field. This interdisciplinary approach illuminates the experiences of neurodiverse employees who have historically been underrepresented in organizational research, while also incorporating sociological insights on how gender roles and expectations shape work-family experiences differently for men and women (Hochschild & Machung, 2012; Daminger, 2019). By examining cognitive differences alongside gendered social dynamics that affect a significant portion of the workforce, this study addresses important gaps in our understanding of workplace inclusion and accommodation.      These aims collectively contribute to both theory and practice. Theoretically, this work expands boundary theory by incorporating executive functioning as a critical individual difference variable, offering a more complete framework for understanding work-family dynamics. Practically, the findings will provide organizations and policymakers with evidence-based guidance for supporting employees with executive dysfunction, potentially improving both employee well-being and organizational outcomes. In the sections that follow, I propose an empirical examination of how executive functioning influences work-family spillover using data from the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS) study. This research will integrate cognitive functioning and work-family literatures, testing hypotheses across two nationally representative samples to understand how executive functioning capabilities shape spillover experiences in the context of gender and social support.
CHAPTER 2Theoretical BackgroundExecutive Functioning      Executive functions (EF) refer to a set of interrelated, yet distinct, higher-order neurocognitive processes that are vital for consciously regulating thoughts, emotions, and goal-directed behavior (Diamond, 2013; Miyake & Friedman, 2012; Snyder et al., 2015). The conceptualization and operationalization of executive functions have been subjects of ongoing debate and research in clinical psychology, cognitive psychology, and neuroscience. Early models of EF posited a single "central executive" responsible for coordinating and delegating various cognitive tasks (Baddeley, 1996). However, more complex models of EF have emerged in recent years. One widely accepted and empirically supported framework is the unity/diversity model proposed by Miyake and Friedman (2012, 2017). This model posits three core executive functions: working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control (Diamond, 2013; Miyake et al., 2000). These functions display "unity" in that they are highly correlated with each other, but "diversity" in that they also show distinct patterns of association with other outcomes and behaviors. Current approaches to modeling EF frequently leverage a bi-factor approach (e.g., Karr et al., 2018), such that an individual's score on an EF task is conceptualized as being jointly caused by a "General EF" dimension, "specific EF" dimensions, non-EF processes, and measurement error (referred to as the “task impurity problem”, see Snyder et al., 2015).      Despite the proliferation of EF models, working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control are three specific EFs present in most frameworks. Working memory involves temporarily holding and manipulating information in an accessible state (Baddeley, 1992; Unsworth & Engle, 2007), allowing individuals to maintain representations of goals, instructions, and potential consequences over time. This capacity is essential for bridging temporal gaps between perceiving information, planning, and acting (D'Esposito & Postle, 2015; Smith & Jonides, 1999). Cognitive flexibility enables switching between different tasks, rules, or mental perspectives (Miyake et al., 2000; Diamond, 2013), helping us adjust behavior in response to changing priorities or situational demands (Ionescu, 2012). Inhibitory control allows us to override automatic responses to execute more appropriate behaviors (Diamond, 2013; Friedman & Miyake, 2004), including resisting distractions and suppressing irrelevant information.      Hofmann et al. (2012) integrated these three components of working memory maintains goals and plans, inhibitory control shields individuals from counterproductive impulses, and cognitive flexibility facilitates adaptive shifting between priorities into a comprehensive model of self-regulation. Research supports this integration, showing that these three executive functions individually contribute to different aspects of self-regulation. For example, studies have found that working memory capacity predicts one's ability to modify situations to avoid temptation, inhibitory control enhances the ability to direct attention away from distractions, and cognitive flexibility supports adapting strategies when faced with changing circumstances or obstacles (Hofmann et al., 2008; McRae et al., 2012; Hendricks & Buchanan, 2016; Garrison & Schmeichel, 2022; Dajani & Uddin, 2015).      This integrated supervisory system enhances broader capacities for attention, reasoning, decision-making, and problem-solving when pursuing goals. Evidence from multiple research domains demonstrates that executive functioning is associated with enhanced self-regulatory capabilities across numerous contexts. For instance, developmental studies show that executive functioning skills in childhood predict greater delay of gratification and impulse control in later life (Moffitt et al., 2011; Shoda et al., 1990), while research on emotion regulation links higher executive functioning to more effective management of negative emotional reactions through strategic cognitive reappraisal (Bridgett et al., 2015).      Prior empirical research has demonstrated that executive functioning uniquely relates to self-regulation and goal attainment in different settings over the life course. For example, Eisenberg et al. (2004) find that preschoolers with better executive functioning skills exhibited greater self-regulation and goal-directed behavior when faced with conflicting desires, such as choosing between completing an academic task or engaging in a more enjoyable activity. Further, research on academic achievement in adolescence has consistently demonstrated that executive functions like working memory and cognitive flexibility are strong predictors of performance across content areas, over and above the effects of general intelligence (Brydges et al., 2012; Lawson et al., 2017; Spiegel et al., 2022; St Clair-Thompson & Gathercole, 2006).       In adulthood, higher levels of executive functioning predict successfully accomplishing and managing competing or interfering role demands and goals. For example, Allan et al. (2016) found that higher executive functioning was associated with increased adherence to health behaviors such as regular physical exercise and smoking cessation. Additionally, adults with higher levels of executive functioning are more successful at pursuing multiple goal domains simultaneously and weigh “alternative responses and their temporally proximal and distal outcomes” (Antshel et al., 2013, p. 109) without experiencing excessive goal interference. Given that the cognitive control enabled by one’s executive functions is a finite resource with a limited capacity that differs from person to person (Kaplan & Berman, 2010), individuals with lower levels of executive functioning may lack the cognitive resources to attend to multiple goals simultaneously, leading to unaccomplished goals. As Masicampo and Baumeister (2011) and Marien et al. (2012) both demonstrate, impaired executive functions can generate a negative feedback loop, such that limited executive functions lead to unaccomplished tasks, these unaccomplished tasks continue to unconsciously occupy one’s attention in the background, thereby reducing the effort and attention one can give to their current tasks.      The negative outcomes that follow from this failed goal pursuit, among other detrimental outcomes of executive dysfunction, have several implications for people in both work and non-work settings. With respect to workplace and labor market outcomes, Küpper et al. (2012) and Barkley et al. (2010) document how adults with executive dysfunction via attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) experience higher rates of job instability, frequent job changes, unemployment, and underemployment compared to their peers without the disorder. Moreover, individuals with executive dysfunction may have difficulty adapting to structured work environments and may experience interpersonal conflicts with colleagues or supervisors due to impulsivity or inattention. These challenges often result in lower income levels and limited career advancement opportunities for those with executive functioning difficulties (Bailey, 2007; Biederman & Faraone, 2006; Fletcher, 2014).      In non-work settings, executive dysfunction can have a significant impact on marriage and family outcomes. Individuals with executive functioning difficulties may struggle with communication, emotional regulation, and maintaining a consistent level of engagement in their relationships (Orlov, 2010). They may experience difficulties in managing household responsibilities, parenting duties, and financial matters, which can lead to increased stress and conflict within the family (Eakin et al., 2004). Murphy and Barkley (1996) show that couples where one or both partners have ADHD may experience higher rates of marital dissatisfaction, separation, and divorce compared to couples without the disorder. Lastly, individuals with lower levels of executive functioning have partners who report experiencing a lower quality of life (Dawson et al. 2015).      These findings converge on the fact that executive functions underlie the self-regulation and management of competing goals and role responsibilities in both work (Chan et al., 2021) and non-work (Zelazo et al., 2024) settings. Individuals with higher levels of EF have the cognitive capacities to juggle multiple pursuits by maintaining focal priorities, ignoring distractions, adjusting their strategies, and avoiding counterproductive impulses that could disrupt goal attainment (Strobach et al., 2018). Individuals with lower levels of EF may struggle to effectively manage multiple priorities and goals across work and personal life domains and are more likely to experience difficulties with attention regulation, goal management, and impulse control (Adler & Shaw, 2011). Consequently, they may experience greater difficulty in successfully navigating the demands of work and life, becoming more prone to role conflicts, reduced goal attainment, and increased stress levels as they struggle to effectively manage competing responsibilities across work and non-work domains (Orlov, 2010). The inability to manage the responsibilities within each domain may establish conditions for negative spillover to occur from work to family life or vice versa and interfere with positive spillover from taking place. In the next section, I provide an overview of spillover, both positive and negative, highlighting the role executive functioning may play with respect to spillover.Work Family Spillover      Work-family spillover occurs when experiences, attitudes, emotions, skills, behaviors, or psychological states are transferred from one domain to another in a way that impacts functioning in the receiving domain (Bhowon, 2013; Grzywacz & Marks, 2000; Hanson et al., 2006). This bi-directional transmission of experiences can take two distinct, yet interrelated, forms: positive spillover that enhances functioning in the receiving domain and negative spillover that impairs functioning in the receiving domain.      Negative Spillover. Negative work-to-family spillover and family-to-work spillover (i.e., “conflict”, see Allen et al., 2023 and Grzywacz & Marks, 2000, p. 7) embodies the depleting, interference-based pathway in which strain, stress, and preoccupation generated in one domain impairs one's capacity to fulfill demands in the other domain. For example, the cognitive fatigue and irritability resulting from excessive work hours may spillover to inhibit an employee's patience and engagement with family activities (Rothbard, 2001).	Several theoretical perspectives on spillover have described the decision-making process individuals engage in when maintaining balance and preventing spillover between domains. These perspectives recognize that individuals are not passive recipients of work and family demands, but active agents who make conscious choices about how to allocate their time, energy, and resources across different life roles. One influential framework in this area is the resource-based perspective proposed by Edwards and Rothbard (2000). This approach suggests that individuals have a finite pool of personal resources such as time, energy, and attention that they must distribute between work and family domains. The allocation of these limited resources involves a series of decisions about how much to invest in each domain, based on factors such as role salience, perceived demands, and anticipated outcomes. Edwards and Rothbard argue that individuals engage in a process of resource allocation that aims to maximize positive outcomes and minimize negative consequences across both domains. Building on this resource-based view, other researchers have explored the cognitive processes involved in work-family decision-making. For example, Greenhaus and Powell (2003) discuss how work-family decision-making and effectively allocating time between domains depends “not only on situational factors (role pressure) but on personal characteristics” (p. 302). Additionally, the boundary management perspective (Ashforth et al., 2000) highlights how individuals make decisions about segmenting or integrating their work and family roles. This approach recognizes that people differ in their preferences for keeping work and family separate or allowing them to blend, and that these preferences inform the strategies they use to manage the boundaries between domains.      Another perspective on decision making and work-family spillover is the selection, optimization, and compensation (SOC) model proposed by Baltes and colleagues (Baltes, 1997; Baltes & Baltes, 1990). The SOC model posits that successful adaptation to changing ecological contexts across the lifespan requires employing strategies of selection (i.e., identifying and prioritizing goals), optimization (i.e., acquiring and refining goal-relevant means), and compensation (i.e., invoking alternative means to maintain functioning in the face of losses). Applied to the work-family interface, the model suggests that individuals must continually select valued goals across both domains, optimize investment of finite personal resources to achieve those aims, and compensate for losses or constraints in one domain by reallocating effort or generating new resources from the other. For instance, an employee might scale back involvement in non-essential work projects to conserve time for family responsibilities (selection), hone time management skills to juggle roles efficiently (optimization) and draw on emotional support from family to replenish resources depleted by workplace demands (compensation). The SOC framework underscores the significant cognitive effort required as individuals engage in the ongoing processes of selecting priorities, optimizing resource allocation, and compensating for constraints across work and family domains.      Building on this perspective, action regulation theory offers another lens through which to understand work-family decision making. While the original theory, developed by Frese and Zapf (1994) and elaborated by Zacher and Frese (2018), explains how individuals generally regulate behavior through cognitive processes like goal development, orientation, planning, monitoring, and feedback processing, Hirschi et al., 2019 developed a distinct model applying these principles specifically to work-family balance. Hirschi et al.’s model conceptualizes balance as the successful joint pursuit of work and family goals through four specific action strategies: allocating resources, changing resources and barriers, sequencing goals, and revising goals. Unlike previous models that focus primarily on the role of demands and resources, Hirschi et al.'s approach emphasizes the individual's agency in shaping their environment and development while uniquely addressing how people manage multiple goals across work and family domains. This model considers how people adaptively use different strategies based on the availability and malleability of resources, the presence of barriers, and the time constraints associated with different goals (Vancouver, Weinhardt, & Schmidt, 2010). This dynamic approach accounts for the complex interplay between work and family domains, acknowledging that strategies may need to be adjusted over time as circumstances change. By focusing on the active pursuit and attainment of work and family goals, this theoretical framework provides insight into how individuals can achieve balance through the flexible use of various goal-oriented strategies. Moreover, it extends the understanding of multiple goal pursuit in the context of work and family roles, addressing a gap in previous research that often focused on competing goals within a single domain (Unsworth et al., 2014).      The decision-making processes these different theories describe demonstrate how cognitively demanding maintaining work and non-work responsibilities can be. For individuals with impaired executive functioning, the challenges of managing work-family spillover may be particularly pronounced. Deficits in executive functioning can limit the cognitive (e.g., difficulty focusing during work conversations) and emotional (e.g., snapping at family after work, see Holley et al., 2017) resources available to an individual, making it harder to effectively allocate these resources between work and family domains and implement strategies these theories describe. Executive functioning deficits impair the brain's ability to regulate attention and emotions, leading to difficulties managing the mental and emotional demands of balancing work and family roles. This can lead to a range of negative consequences for both the individual, their family, and their workplace, including increased stress, reduced performance in both domains, and diminished overall well-being.      To my knowledge, no studies to date have directly examined the relationship between executive functioning and work-family outcomes, though research on adjacent constructs suggests this is an important area for investigation (e.g., ADHD and labor market outcomes: Fletcher, 2014; Küpper et al., 2012; ADHD and family conflict: Eakin et al., 2004; Johnston & Mash, 2001). Lower levels of executive function (i.e., executive dysfunction) may result in negative work-family outcomes (i.e., negative work-to-family and family-to-work spillover) due to difficulties in managing time effectively within a given domain. Additionally, positive spillover can go from the family domain to the work domain and vice versa (Grzywacz & Carlson, 2007). For example, positive work-to-family spillover may occur when an employee who gains leadership and interpersonal abilities from their workplace responsibilities leverages those competencies to become a more effective parent. Further, positive family-to-work spillover may occur if the time an employee spends with their family recharges and energizes them for the following workday.       Positive Spillover. Higher executive functioning may facilitate positive work-family outcomes (i.e., positive work-to-family and family-to-work spillover) by allowing individuals to extract skills, perspectives, emotions, and other resources from one domain that enhance their functioning in another. For example, the cognitive flexibility inherent in executive functioning could enable an employee to fluidly shift between the analytical mindset required at work and the emotional attentiveness demanded by parenting, allowing skills and positive affect from one role to enrich the other rather than compete with it (Ferguson et al., 2015). Similarly, inhibitory control may help employees set aside work-related preoccupations, freeing cognitive resources to actively engage with and apply professionally developed interpersonal skills in family interactions. Working memory capacity may further support positive spillover by enabling individuals to hold and transfer complex strategies across domains—for example, maintaining awareness of effective collaboration techniques from work and deliberately applying them to family problem-solving. More broadly, the planning and organizational abilities associated with executive functioning may allow individuals to structure their time in ways that create opportunities for enrichment, such as proactively scheduling meaningful family activities that benefit from the energy and confidence generated during a productive workday. Overall, higher levels of executive functioning have the potential to enhance individuals' capacity to recognize transferable resources across roles and actively deploy them, promoting enriching integration across work and family domains (Lapierre et al., 2018). Given the different societal expectations men and women face when it comes to dealing with the boundaries between work and non-work, there is reason to believe that gender may moderate the relationship between executive functioning and spillover, primarily through the increased household management demands expected of women. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that there are no gender differences in executive functioning (Grissom & Reyes, 2019) or work-family conflict (Shockley et al., 2017). However, traditional gender roles and societal expectations often place a greater burden on women to manage household and caregiving responsibilities, even when they are employed full-time (Hochschild & Machung, 2012). This unequal distribution of domestic labor can lead to increased strain on women's cognitive resources, making them more vulnerable to the negative effects of executive dysfunction on work-family spillover (Ciciolla & Luthar, 2019).      Women frequently report spending more time on 'invisible labor' (e.g., household managerial tasks and childcare, see Lareau & Weininger, 2008) compared to men, regardless of their employment status (Bianchi et al., 2012; Offer & Schneider, 2011). This 'second shift' of domestic work can be mentally and physically taxing, leaving women with less time and energy to devote to their professional responsibilities. Moreover, women more often take on the cognitive dimension of household labor, which involves the mental effort required to plan, organize, and manage household tasks (Daminger, 2019; Offer & Schneider, 2011). These disproportionate demands on women's cognitive resources may mean that the relationship between executive functioning and work-family spillover is stronger for women than for men (Eby et al., 2005; Nadkarni & Biswas, 2022). That is, women may need to draw more heavily on cognitive flexibility and inhibitory control to manage frequent role transitions (Allen et al., 2015; Kossek & Lautsch, 2012), making individual differences in executive functioning a more consequential predictor of their spillover outcomes.      Research by Daminger (2019), Dean et al. (2022), and Wayne et al. (2023) demonstrates that women bear a disproportionate share of cognitive labor and mental load in household management and the byproducts of such labor are less tangible than their male partners. Their findings show that women take on more anticipation and monitoring tasks at home, creating an additional cognitive burden beyond physical domestic duties. This disparity may be further reinforced by gender role socialization, as women often internalize expectations to maintain equal engagement across both domains (Glavin et al., 2011; Offer, 2014), compounding the cognitive demands placed on their executive functioning resources. Given that executive functioning is responsible for cognitive control processes like attention, working memory, and task-switching, the increased cognitive demands that women face are likely to tax these functions more heavily. Based on these findings, I propose that the greater cognitive demands women face may amplify the importance of executive functioning in managing work-family boundaries, such that individual differences in executive functioning are a stronger predictor of spillover outcomes for women than for men.      The cognitive demands on women likely magnify the consequences of executive functioning challenges, particularly for negative spillover in both directions. Women with lower executive functioning may face compounded difficulties when managing the numerous transitions between work and family domains, as they must navigate more complex and frequent role transitions throughout the day. The added cognitive burden women face from household management may deplete the executive resources needed to prevent negative spillover, both from work to family and from family to work. For negative family-to-work spillover, women's greater responsibility for anticipating and addressing family needs may make it particularly difficult to compartmentalize family concerns during work hours when executive functioning is compromised. Similarly, for negative work-to-family spillover, women with lower executive functioning may struggle more than men to disengage from work thoughts and responsibilities during family time, as they must simultaneously manage their greater share of household cognitive labor. On the one hand, women's greater involvement in managing both work and family domains could provide more opportunities for skills, perspectives, and positive experiences to transfer between roles (Offer & Schneider, 2011; Powell & Greenhaus, 2010). Their heightened engagement across domains might allow women with stronger executive functioning to more readily identify and capitalize on instances where resources from one domain could benefit the other. Conversely, the increased cognitive load women face might make it more challenging to recognize and leverage positive spillover opportunities, even with robust executive functioning capabilities. For men, traditional gender roles that compartmentalize work and family responsibilities may limit positive spillover by narrowing the range of cross-domain experiences available for transfer. However, this same compartmentalization could paradoxically enhance positive spillover when it does occur, as clearer psychological boundaries between roles may make it easier to recognize and deliberately apply resources gained in one domain to the other (Rothbard & Edwards, 2003; Swanberg, 2004). The complexity of these potential mechanisms, combined with the lack of clear empirical evidence for gender differences in positive spillover, makes this an important area for exploration. Understanding how gender moderates the relationship between executive functioning and positive spillover could reveal unexplored pathways for promoting beneficial work-family interactions across gender identities. Studies examining the work-family interface have established that access to resources in both work and family domains significantly shapes how individuals experience work-family conflict (Allen et al., 2000), spillover (Grzywacz & Marks, 2000), and enrichment (Greenhaus & Powell, 2006), ultimately affecting both personal and organizational outcomes. Specifically, the availability of support in either domain plays a critical role in determining how effectively individuals navigate work-family demands. This perspective is grounded in several theories, such as conservation of resources theory (COR; Hobfoll, 1989) which posits that individuals strive to obtain, retain, protect, and foster resources that they value, and stress occurs when there is a threat of resource loss, an actual loss, or a lack of expected gain in resources following resource investment. Additionally, the job demands-resources model (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007) points out that job resources, such as social support, can buffer the impact of job demands on strain, including work-family conflict. Parallel to the work-family literature, the availability of support as a resource has been frequently shown to benefit individuals with cognitive deficits and difficulties with executive functioning (Diamond, 2013b; Tun et al., 2013; Sims et al., 2011). For example, Seeman et al. (2001) find that social support can compensate for executive function impairments in older adults, enabling them to maintain daily functioning that would otherwise be compromised by cognitive deficits. Similarly, supportive environments help children with ADHD better manage behavioral and academic demands despite their executive functioning difficulties (Langberg et al., 2011). In both cases, support does not simply improve cognition in isolation but rather attenuates the negative consequences of executive dysfunction on real-world outcomes—a pattern consistent with a moderation framework in which support weakens the link between cognitive deficits and functional impairment. Taken together, these findings suggest that support may be particularly important for individuals with lower levels of executive functioning in managing spillover between work and non-work domains, making it crucial to understand how different forms of support manifest across various life contexts.      Within the organizational domain, support can take multiple forms, from formal policies to day-to-day interpersonal interactions. Informal support within organizations primarily comes from supervisors and coworkers, with supervisors often having greater ability to implement policies and provide resources that directly affect an employee's ability to manage work and family demands (Thompson & Prottas, 2006). Empirical evidence from Clark et al. (2017) highlights the importance of a supportive environment in mitigating the negative effects of work and family stressors on work-family outcomes, finding that informal support factors were generally more impactful than formal organizational support factors such as flextime and telecommuting. Drawing from boundary theory (Ashforth et al., 2000), which explains how individuals create, maintain, and cross the psychological boundaries between different life domains such as work and family, a supportive environment can provide individuals with additional resources to manage the demands of work and family roles effectively. These environments may help compensate for the deficits caused by EF limitations (Williams et al., 2009). For example, supportive supervisors and coworkers might provide additional structure and clear deadlines for employees who have difficulty with planning and organization. Similarly, family support systems might include partners who help create and maintain routines, or who assist with breaking down complex household tasks into manageable steps for those who struggle with executive planning. Various forms of informal support have the potential to buffer against the negative effects of EF deficits on work-family spillover, reducing instances of negative spillover such as work-related distractions during family time or family-related stress affecting work performance (Allen et al., 2015; Carlson & Perrewé, 1999; Ferguson et al., 2016).      Supervisor support is defined as the extent to which supervisors 'provide encouragement and support to employees within their work groups' (Griffin et al., 2001, p. 537). It includes emotional support, instrumental support, role modeling, and creative work-family management (Hammer et al., 2009). Supervisor support, particularly family-supportive supervisor behaviors (FSSB), has been consistently linked to reduced work-family conflict and increased work-family enrichment (Kossek et al., 2011). For individuals with lower executive functioning, supervisor support may be especially valuable as a compensatory resource. Supervisors who provide clear expectations, consistent communication, and structured workflows can reduce the cognitive demands of the work role for employees who may struggle with planning and self-regulation (Adamou et al., 2013; Barkley, 2012). Drawing from the job demands-resources model (Bakker & Demerouti, 2007), supervisor support can be conceptualized as a job resource that buffers the impact of executive function deficits on work-family spillover. This support may help mitigate negative work-to-family spillover by reducing work-related strain that would otherwise carry over into the home, and may also attenuate negative family-to-work spillover by providing a more structured and forgiving work environment that accommodates employees whose family demands have depleted their cognitive resources. Thus, I hypothesize the following:H7a: Supervisor support will moderate the relationship between executive functioning and negative family-to-work spillover, such that the negative relationship between executive functioning and negative family-to-work spillover will be weaker (i.e. less negative) as levels of supervisor support increase.H7b: Supervisor support will moderate the relationship between executive functioning and negative work-to-family spillover, such that the negative relationship between executive functioning and negative work-to-family spillover will be weaker (i.e. less negative) as levels of supervisor support increase.      While the buffering effects of supervisor support on negative spillover are well-documented, the role of supervisor support in facilitating positive spillover for individuals with varying levels of executive function is less clear. For positive spillover, supportive supervision may enhance the transfer of positive experiences and resources between domains through multiple mechanisms. For instance, supportive supervision could help individuals with lower EF better recognize and leverage their unique strengths, potentially leading to increased confidence that transfers between domains. Additionally, when supervisors provide structured guidance and positive feedback, this may help employees develop compensatory strategies that could benefit both work and family roles. However, the exact nature of these relationships remains unclear, particularly given the complex interplay between executive function and resource utilization. Therefore, I propose the following research questions regarding supervisor support, executive functioning, and positive spillover:RQ3: Does supervisor support moderate the relationship between EF and positive family-to-work spillover?RQ4: Does supervisor support moderate the relationship between EF and positive work-to-family spillover?      Coworker support refers to the assistance, encouragement, and resources provided by peers in the workplace. It encompasses multiple dimensions, including instrumental (e.g., helping with a work task) and affective (e.g., listening to a colleague's concerns) support (Chiaburu & Harrison, 2008). For individuals with executive dysfunction, coworker support can be particularly beneficial in managing workplace challenges. Coworkers can provide instrumental support by helping to break down complex tasks, offering reminders for deadlines, and assisting with prioritization (Biederman et al., 2006). This type of support can compensate for deficits in working memory and organization skills often associated with executive dysfunction. For individuals with lower EF, supportive coworkers might help create an environment where experimentation with new organizational or time management strategies feels safe, potentially leading to the development of skills that benefit both domains. Additionally, the emotional support and camaraderie from coworkers might boost self-efficacy and positive affect, resources that could transfer between work and family domains. However, the specific ways these manifest for individuals with different levels of executive function requires further investigation. Therefore, I propose the following research questions regarding supervisor support, executive functioning, and positive spillover:RQ5: Does coworker support moderate the relationship between EF and positive family-to-work spillover?RQ6: Does coworker support moderate the relationship between EF and positive work-to-family spillover?      Within the family domain, social support primarily comes from spouses (Michel et al., 2011). Spousal support is defined as the emotional, instrumental, and appraisal assistance provided by one's partner in a marriage or committed relationship (Burleson, 2003). Spousal support plays a crucial role in managing work-family demands (King et al., 1995) and has been identified as critical for marital satisfaction and reducing family-to-work conflict (Michel et al., 2011). For individuals with executive dysfunction, spousal support can be invaluable in managing both work and home life. The challenges associated with executive dysfunction often extend beyond the workplace, affecting home organization, time management, and relationship dynamics (Barkley et al., 2008). A supportive spouse can provide crucial assistance in areas such as household management, financial planning, and schedule coordination, which can be particularly challenging for individuals with executive function deficits (Robin, 2016).      Drawing from the work-home resources model (Brummelhuis & Bakker, 2012), spousal support can be conceptualized as a home resource that can buffer the impact of executive function deficits on work-family spillover. For negative spillover, a supportive spouse can help create structure and routines at home, potentially reducing stress and improving overall functioning in both domains (Pera, 2008). Emotional support from a spouse can also help mitigate the frustration and self-doubt often experienced by individuals with executive dysfunction (Ramsay, 2017). This emotional and instrumental support may help compensate for executive function deficits, potentially reducing negative spillover between work and family domains, particularly for those with lower levels of executive functioning. Supportive spouses might help individuals with lower EF develop and implement effective strategies for managing daily responsibilities, leading to increased mastery experiences that could benefit both domains. Additionally, the emotional security and confidence fostered by a supportive spouse might enhance an individual's ability to recognize and capitalize on positive experiences across domains. Spouses might also help individuals with lower EF better identify and leverage their strengths, potentially facilitating the transfer of positive experiences and resources between work and family roles. CHAPTER 3Methods      Sample and Procedures      This study will use data from the second and third waves of the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS) study, collected between 2004-2006 and 2012-2014, respectively. The MIDUS study is a national longitudinal research project focused on understanding factors that influence health and well-being during midlife and beyond. All analyses will be conducted on both samples to ensure the robustness of this study's findings.       The MIDUS study’s sampling design employed a stratified, random-digit-dial (RDD) sampling frame to obtain a nationally representative sample of non-institutionalized, English-speaking adults from the 48 contiguous United States. The sampling procedure used a multistage probability design with stratification by geographic region and metropolitan statistical areas (MSA) to ensure adequate representation across different demographic segments. The original MIDUS study (Wave 1: 1995-1996) established the baseline cohort, with subsequent waves following up with these participants over time.      The second wave of the MIDUS survey (MIDUS II) included n = 4,963 non-institutionalized adults aged 32-84, representing a 75% retention rate from the original sample. MIDUS II data collection involved a comprehensive, multimodal assessment protocol that included telephone interviews (approximately 30 minutes) followed by self-administered questionnaires (SAQs) mailed to participants (taking approximately 2 hours to complete). Wave 3 (MIDUS III) included n = 3,294 non-institutionalized adults from the Wave 2 sample, representing a 66% retention rate between survey waves.      For the present investigation, final analyses will be performed on a subsample of MIDUS participants who 1) employed at the time of data collection and 2) have complete data on all variables of interest. This selection ensures that participants have the potential to experience both work and family role demands. All measures described below were assessed at both MIDUS 2 and MIDUS 3.Measures      Executive Functioning. Executive functioning will be assessed using a subset of tests from the Brief Test of Adult Cognition by Telephone (BTACT; Lachman et al., 2014). The BTACT is a comprehensive cognitive battery designed to be administered over the telephone in approximately 20 minutes. The executive functioning factor of the BTACT consists of five tests out of the seven contained in the BTACT (excluding the immediate and delayed word list recall tasks, which measure episodic memory rather than executive functioning):1. The Backward Digit Span measures working memory by having participants repeat increasingly long strings of digits in reverse order (Wechsler, 1997).2. Category Fluency measures verbal fluency and executive function by having participants generate as many words as possible from a given category (e.g., animals) in 1 minute (Borkowski et al., 1967; Tombaugh et al., 1999).3. Number Series measures inductive reasoning by having participants determine the next number in a series based on a pattern (Salthouse & Prill, 1987).4. The 30-Seconds and Counting Task (30-SACT) measures processing speed by having participants count backwards from 100 as quickly as possible for 30 seconds (Tun & Lachman, 2008).5. The Stop and Go Switch Task (SGST) measures task-switching ability and reaction time in an executive function task involving switching between rules for verbal responses (Tun & Lachman, 2008).      In the present study, executive functioning will be operationalized using a composite score derived from multiple BTACT exercises: the mean of z-scores for Digits Backward, Category Fluency, Number Series, Backward Counting, and the mean of switch and nonswitch trials in the Stop & Go Switch Task. This composite score is computed following the scoring procedure outlined by the Tun and Lachman (2012). Both the individual exercise responses as well as the pre-computed composite score are available in the MIDUS database. Thus, the executive functioning composite scores to be used in this analysis will be those calculated by the MIDUS research team following their established protocol rather than computed independently for this study. This approach of using composite scores helps address the concerns regarding the task impurity problem mentioned earlier, as combining multiple measures into latent variables or factor scores has been shown to considerably improve measurement reliability and validity compared to using individual tasks or difference scores alone (Enkavi et al., 2019). The psychometric structure of the BTACT has previously been validated in prior studies (Gurnani et al., 2015; Tun et al., 2006) and, although the MIDUS documentation does not report reliability statistics for the BTACT, Lachman et al. (2014) report alternate-forms reliability coefficients for the executive functioning factor ranging from .80 to .88 and test-retest reliability ranging from .80 to .86 across two studies (see Table 7), indicating strong reliability and temporal stability of the measure.      Spillover. Positive and negative family-to-work and work-to-family spillover will be measured on a five-point frequency scale ranging from 1 (“All the time”) to 5 (“Never”) using the scale developed by Grzywacz and colleagues (Grzywacz, 2000; Grzywacz & Marks, 2000a; Grzywacz & Marks, 2000b; Grzywacz & Marks, 2001) that is included in all panels of the MIDUS study. Sample items for negative work-to-family spillover include: “Stress at work makes you irritable at home” and “Job worries or problems distract you when you are at home”. Sample items for negative family-to-work spillover include: “Responsibilities at home reduce the effort you can devote to your job” and “Stress at home makes you irritable at work”. Sample items for positive work-to-family spillover include: “The things you do at work help you deal with personal and practical issues at home.” and “The skills you use on your job are useful for things you have to do at home.”. Sample items for positive family-to-work spillover include: “Providing for what is needed at home makes you work harder at your job.” and “Your home life helps you relax and feel ready for the next day’s work.”.  Reliability values for the four scales in the full sample ranged from 0.71 to 0.82. This work-family spillover scale has been used extensively in prior empirical work and has been validated against common measures of WFC (e.g., Netemeyer et al., 1996, see Table A1 of Allen et al., 2023 for more information).      Coworker support. Coworker support will be measured using items originally developed for the Whitehall Health Survey of British Civil Servants (Marmot et al., 1991), which were later incorporated as a subscale within the larger job characteristics scale within MIDUS. Participants are asked to rate the extent to which they agree with the given statements using five-point frequency scale, ranging from 1 (“All the time”) to 5 (“Never”). The items include “How often do you get help and support from your coworkers” and “How often are your coworkers willing to listen to your work-related problems.” Scores will be reverse-coded, such that higher scores will reflect higher amounts of support. Reliability values for coworker support were 0.67 in the second wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample and 0.95 in the third wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample.      Supervisor support. Supervisor support will be measured using items originally developed for the Whitehall Health Survey of British Civil Servants (Marmot et al., 1991), which were later incorporated as a subscale within the larger job characteristics scale in MIDUS. Participants are asked to rate the extent to which they agree with the given statements using five-point frequency scale, ranging from 1 (“All the time”) to 5 (“Never”). The items include “How often do you get the information you need from your supervisor or superiors,” “How often do you get help and support from your immediate supervisor,” and “How often is your immediate supervisor willing to listen to your work-related problems.”. Scores will be reverse-coded, such that higher scores will reflect higher amounts of support. Reliability values for coworker support were 0.87 in the second wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample and 0.97 in the third wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample.      Spousal Support. Spousal support will be measured using the Social Support and Strain Scale, which assesses the perception of caring and understanding an individual receives from their family, friends, and partners (Walen & Lachman, 2000). This measure has been used extensively in prior empirical studies examining spousal support with the MIDUS sample (Donoho et al., 2015; Grzywacz & Marks, 2000, 2001; Jiao & Grzywavz, 2023; Ulichney et al., 2023) Items include: “How much does your spouse or partner really care about you?”, “How much does he or she understand the way you feel about things?”, “How much does he or she appreciate you?”, “How much do you rely on him or her for help if you have a serious problem?”, “How much can you open up to him or her if you need to talk about your worries?”, and “How much can you relax and be yourself around him or her?” Each item was scored on a 1 (“A lot.”) to 4 (“Not at all.”). Scores will be constructed by calculating the mean of the values of the items in each scale. Items will be reverse-coded so that high scores reflect higher levels of received spousal support. Reliability values for spousal support were 0.90 in the second wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample and 0.91 in the third wave of the MIDUS study’s main sample.      Control Variables      Job Demands. Job demands will be included following the approach used by Allen et al. (2023) who use the job demands subscale from the larger job characteristics scale included in MIDUS. Participants are asked to rate the extent to which they agree with the given statements using five-point frequency scale, ranging from 1 ("All the time") to 5 ("Never"). Job demands items measure the extent to which a participant finds his or her job responsibilities to be demanding. Items include how often: "Do you have to work very intensively – that is, you are very busy trying to get things done?"; "Do different people or groups at work demand things from you that you think are hard to combine?"; "(How often) do you have too many demands made on you."; "(How often) you have enough time to get everything done?"; "(How often) you have a lot of interruption?" The composite score will be constructed by calculating the sum of the reverse-coded values of the items in the scale. Reliability values for job demands were 0.73 in the second wave of the MIDUS study's main sample and 0.76 in the third wave of the MIDUS study's main sample. Job demands are included as a covariate to account for the well-established direct relationship between demands and spillover outcomes (e.g., Bakker & Demerouti, 2007). However, because job demands could plausibly function as part of the pathway linking executive functioning to spillover, and because some content overlap exists between demand and spillover items, sensitivity analyses examine whether results differ when job demands are excluded from the models (see Multiverse Analysis, Arm A). Additionally, Arm B of the multiverse analysis examines whether job demands moderate the relationship between executive functioning and spillover, directly testing whether demands play a substantive role in the model beyond serving as a covariate.      Family Demands. Family demands will be computed using the strategy by Allen et al., (2023) who combined three items measuring time/stress demands at home: (“You have too many demands made on you.”, “You have enough time to get everything done.”, “You have a lot of interruptions.”) with two items measuring family support (“Not including your spouse or partner, how often do members of your family make too many demands on you?”,  “How often does your spouse or partner make too many demands on you?”). The three items measuring Time/Stress Demands at Home are based on a five-point scale that ranged from “All the time” (1) to “Never” (5). The two-items measuring family support are based on a four-point scale that ranged from “Often” (1) to “Never” (4). Given the different number of scale points across the items, items will be transformed using the percent of maximum possible (POMP) method (Cohen et al., 1999): POMP = 100 ? [(Observed score ? Minimum possible)⁄(Maximum possible ? Minimum possible)]. Using this method, the minimum and maximum scores across items will be equivalent (i.e., 0 and 100, respectively). Additionally, responses will be reverse-scored when appropriate, such that higher scores will indicate greater family demands.      In addition to family and job demands, household-level variables that will be controlled for in all analyses include household income and household size. Given the affordances income can have on against executive functioning deficits (Duncan et al., 2017) as well as the increased demands a larger household can place on an individual, controlling for these factors will aid in isolating the effect of executive functioning on spillover. Individual-level variables that will be controlled for in all analyses include age, gender, and education level (measured on a 12-point scale from "No school/some grade school" to "Ph.D./Professional degree", capturing key milestones like high school graduation, associate's, bachelor's, and master's degrees).      Analytic Strategy      All hypotheses were tested using standard multiple linear regression. The interaction was not significant for negative family-to-work (B = 0.026, p = .558), positive family-to-work (B = ?0.071, p = .213), or positive work-to-family spillover (B = ?0.050, p = .382).      In MIDUS 3 (Table 12), the EF ? Coworker support maintained significant main effects on negative spillover in expected directions. In summary, H9 and H10 were not consistently supported, and RQ5 and RQ6 revealed no replicable coworker support moderation effects across waves.      Hypotheses 11 and 12 predicted that spousal support would buffer the negative relationship between executive functioning and negative spillover. Research questions 7 and 8 explored whether spousal support moderated the relationship between executive functioning and positive spillover. Results are presented in Tables 13 and 14.      In MIDUS 2 (Table 13), the EF ? Spousal Support interaction was not significant for any of the four spillover outcomes (all ps > .05). Spousal support demonstrated significant main effects, most notably a strong positive association with positive family-to-work spillover (R? = .195 for positive family-to-work in this model). Of the two executive functioning–related terms that reached significance in MIDUS 2 (the EF conditional main effect and the EF ? Social support variables—supervisor, coworker, and spousal—demonstrated significant main effects in expected directions across most models, even though they did not consistently moderate the executive functioning–spillover relationship. The primary finding is that executive functioning, as measured by the BTACT composite, did not demonstrate robust main or interactive effects on work-family spillover. None of the 12 directional hypotheses received consistent support across waves. Several isolated significant effects emerged in one wave but did not replicate in the other, limiting confidence in their robustness.
CHAPTER 6Discussion      Neurodiversity in the workplace has received increased attention in the organizational sciences (Lefevre-Levy et al., 2023). While much of this focus has examined behavior within the work domain, it is important to recognize that the boundaries between work and non-work are permeable and subject to carry-over effects when transitioning between domains. This research sought to elucidate the relationship between executive functioning and work-family spillover, as well as examine the moderating effects of gender and social support on this relationship. This research is particularly important given that neurocognitive deficits represent the most common type of disability among working adults in the United States (CDC, 2023). Across 40 regression models tested in two independent samples of dual-earner couples from the Midlife in the United States study (MIDUS II: N = 932; MIDUS III: N = 507), the results overwhelmingly did not support the hypothesized relationships between executive functioning and work-family spillover. The findings, their implications for theory and practice, and the boundary conditions of this research are discussed below.Summary of Findings      Hypotheses 1 through 4 predicted that executive functioning would be negatively related to negative spillover (H1: work-to-family; H2: family-to-work) and positively related to positive spillover (H3: work-to-family; H4: family-to-work). Of these four hypotheses tested across two waves, only one statistically significant effect emerged: in MIDUS II, executive functioning was negatively related to positive family-to-work spillover (B = -0.222, ? = -.074, p < .05). This finding was in the opposite direction of what H4 predicted, suggesting that individuals with higher executive functioning reported less positive transfer from family to work. This effect was small in magnitude and did not replicate in MIDUS III (B = -0.140, ? = -.032, p = .48). The remaining tests of H1 through H4 across both waves were nonsignificant, with standardized coefficients ranging from ? = -.074 to ? = .060, all well below the minimum detectable effect sizes established in the sensitivity power analysis (? ≥ .08 for MIDUS II; ? ≥ .11 for MIDUS III).      Hypotheses 5 and 6 predicted that gender would moderate the relationship between executive functioning and negative spillover, and research questions 1 and 2 explored gender moderation for positive spillover. None of the gender ? executive functioning interactions were statistically significant in either wave. Interaction coefficients were uniformly small (|?| ranging from .003 to .243) and inconsistent in direction across waves.      The social support moderation hypotheses (H7–H12) and research questions (RQ3–RQ8) examined whether supervisor, coworker, and spousal support buffered the relationship between executive functioning and spillover. Of the 24 interaction tests across two waves, only three reached statistical significance, and none replicated. In MIDUS II, the executive functioning ? supervisor support interaction was significant for positive family-to-work spillover (B = -0.085, ? = -.320, p < .05), but this interaction was not significant in MIDUS III (? = .162, p = .38). In MIDUS III, the EF ? supervisor support interaction was significant for positive work-to-family spillover (B = 0.235, ? = .605, p < .001), but this effect was not significant in MIDUS II (? = -.246, p = .07) and was in the opposite direction. No coworker support or spousal support interactions were significant in either wave.      Cross-wave replicability, assessed using the Open Science Collaboration (2015) criteria, was poor. Of the three significant effects observed in MIDUS II, only one showed the same directional sign in MIDUS III (Step 1: 33.3%), none were also significant in MIDUS III (Step 2: 0%), and the one effect significant in both waves showed opposite signs with non-overlapping confidence intervals (Step 3: 0%). The multiverse analyses confirmed this pattern: across alternative specifications—including models with no covariates, demographics only, education as SES proxy, and covariates modeled as moderators—the executive functioning coefficient was overwhelmingly nonsignificant, and the few significant effects that emerged were inconsistent across waves and specifications. Taken together, the pattern of results suggests that the few significant effects observed are likely Type I errors, consistent with what would be expected by chance given the number of tests conducted.Theoretical Implications      The null main effects are notable given the theoretical rationale for expecting executive functioning to play a role in managing work-family boundaries. Conservation of resources theory (Hobfoll, 1989) suggests that individuals with greater cognitive resources should be better equipped to manage cross-domain demands, and work-family border theory (Clark, 2000) posits that effective boundary management requires the kinds of planning, inhibition, and cognitive flexibility that executive functions support. However, the present findings suggest that the general cognitive capacities captured by the BTACT composite may not operate as the proximal mechanisms through which individuals manage work-family spillover.      First, the BTACT measures a broad composite of cognitive abilities including working memory, category fluency, processing speed, and task switching. While these abilities are related to the executive functioning construct, they may be too distal from the day-to-day self-regulatory processes that govern spillover experiences. The specific cognitive mechanisms theorized to matter most for work-family management—such as emotion regulation, goal prioritization, and attentional control in the face of competing demands—may not be well captured by a telephone-administered cognitive battery. Diamond (2013) and Hofmann et al. (2012) distinguish between foundational executive capacities and the downstream self-regulatory applications of those capacities; the present findings suggest it is the latter that may be more directly relevant to spillover.      Importantly, the ways in which low executive functioning manifests in everyday work and family life may not be well captured by the discrete, structured tasks that comprise the BTACT. In daily life, executive functioning difficulties often present as challenges with planning and sequencing multi-step activities (e.g., coordinating childcare logistics while preparing for a work deadline), difficulty disengaging from one role’s demands when transitioning to another (e.g., ruminating about a workplace conflict during family dinner), poor time estimation and chronic lateness, trouble prioritizing competing obligations, and emotional reactivity under stress that spills across domains. These real-world manifestations involve complex, context-dependent interactions among cognitive, emotional, and motivational processes that unfold over extended time periods. The BTACT’s constituent tasks—such as recalling digit strings backward, generating words within a category, or completing number series—are brief, decontextualized cognitive exercises that assess the capacity for executive control under standardized conditions but may not reflect how effectively individuals deploy that capacity amid the messy, emotionally charged demands of managing work and family roles simultaneously. This gap between laboratory-assessed capacity and real-world executive functioning performance has been noted in the neuropsychological literature more broadly (Chaytor & Schmitter-Edgecombe, 2003; Sbordone, 2008).      Relatedly, the clinical conditions most strongly characterized by executive dysfunction—particularly attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)—have very low base rates in the MIDUS sample. ADHD prevalence among adults is estimated at 4–5% in the general population (Kessler et al., 2006), and this rate is likely even lower among the MIDUS participants who completed both the survey and cognitive assessments, given that study participation itself requires the organizational and follow-through capacities that individuals with significant executive dysfunction may struggle with. The resulting restriction of range at the lower end of the executive functioning distribution means that the sample largely captures normal variation in cognitive ability among relatively high-functioning adults, rather than the kinds of clinically meaningful executive deficits that theories of cognitive resource depletion and boundary management failure most directly implicate. Prior research linking executive dysfunction to adverse outcomes in adjacent domains—such as the associations between ADHD and labor market difficulties (Fletcher, 2014; Küpper et al., 2012) or family conflict (Eakin et al., 2004)—has typically examined individuals with diagnosed conditions, not the subclinical variation present in a community sample. The absence of significant effects in the present study may therefore reflect a floor effect: executive functioning may only meaningfully predict spillover when it falls below a functional threshold that few MIDUS participants reach.      Second, the between-person design of this study may obscure within-person processes that are more relevant to spillover. Work-family spillover is inherently a dynamic, episodic process that unfolds within days and across role transitions. An individual’s average level of executive functioning, measured at a single time point, may not capture the momentary fluctuations in cognitive resources that drive spillover experiences on a given day.      Third, the null findings may reflect the strength of situational and contextual factors relative to individual cognitive differences in determining spillover. Job demands (? = .40–.50 for negative work-to-family spillover) and family demands (? = .37–.45 for negative family-to-work spillover) were far more potent predictors of spillover than individual differences in executive functioning across all models. This is consistent with a situation strength perspective (Mischel, 1977; Meyer et al., 2010), wherein strong situational demands constrain the influence of individual differences on behavior and outcomes. Executive functioning may matter more in weak situations where individuals have greater latitude in how they manage boundaries, a possibility that the present between-person design could not test.      The lack of gender moderation contributes to a growing body of evidence suggesting that the predictors of work-family spillover may be more similar across genders than different (Shockley et al., 2017). While gendered disparities in work-family experiences certainly exist, these disparities may be driven more by differential exposure to demands and resources—such as structural inequities in household labor distribution and workplace flexibility—than by differential vulnerability to cognitive resource limitations.Practical Implications      This research has significant implications for individuals’ overall well-being and their ability to manage work-family boundaries. The original motivation for this study was that individuals with strong executive functioning might experience less spillover due to more efficient cognitive resource allocation, better multitasking, and more effective decision-making across domains. However, the results suggest that individual differences in broad executive functioning do not meaningfully predict spillover. Instead, the practical implications of this research derive primarily from identifying which factors did consistently predict spillover across both waves and all models.      The consistent, large effects of job demands on negative work-to-family spillover (? = .40–.50) suggest that organizations seeking to reduce spillover should focus on workload management. Redesigning jobs to manage workload intensity—through strategies such as realistic workload expectations, adequate staffing, and autonomy over task scheduling—may be among the most effective interventions. Similarly, the strong effects of family demands on negative family-to-work spillover (? = .37–.45) underscore the value of organizational policies that accommodate family responsibilities, such as flexible scheduling, dependent care support, and family leave policies.      At the organizational level, the findings underscore the importance of social support in work-family dynamics. The main effects of supervisor support, coworker support, and spousal support were consistent with prior literature across both waves: each was negatively associated with negative spillover and positively associated with positive spillover. These findings are consistent with French et al.’s (2018) meta-analytic findings and suggest that social support operates as a direct resource for managing work-family demands. Organizations seeking to reduce work-family spillover and foster a more supportive work environment should consider investing in supervisor training for family-supportive behaviors (Hammer et al., 2011), fostering supportive team norms, and creating structures that facilitate coworker support. Furthermore, human resource practices such as job crafting, role design, or i-deals could be tailored to address the situational factors—demands and support—that this study identified as the primary drivers of spillover, rather than targeting individual cognitive characteristics.      For organizations concerned about supporting employees with executive functioning difficulties or neurocognitive disabilities, the present findings suggest that cognitive screening or cognitive training programs are unlikely to meaningfully reduce work-family spillover. Instead, the environmental supports already identified in the neurodiversity literature may be more productive. Flexible work arrangements, such as remote work options and adjustable schedules, accommodate the unique needs of employees with executive functioning challenges while benefiting all employees. Assistive technologies and tools to support task management, time tracking, and goal setting can help individuals with executive functioning difficulties stay organized and focused. Implementing mentorship programs and fostering a supportive and inclusive workplace culture can also contribute to the success of employees with diverse cognitive abilities. Notably, these policies would not only benefit those with executive functioning difficulties but all employees holistically, as they promote a more accommodating and empathetic work environment (Lefevre-Levy et al., 2023). The key insight from the present study is that these environmental and structural supports are likely effective not because they compensate for cognitive deficits that drive spillover, but because they directly address the situational demands and support deficits that actually do.Limitations and Future Directions      Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the BTACT is a telephone-administered cognitive assessment designed for population-level research, and while it has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties (Lachman et al., 2014), it may lack the sensitivity to capture the specific executive functioning processes most relevant to work-family boundary management. As discussed above, the gap between what the BTACT measures and how executive functioning difficulties actually manifest in the daily management of work and family roles may be substantial. Future research would benefit from measures that more closely approximate the real-world cognitive demands of boundary management, such as performance-based assessments that simulate competing work and family task demands or ecological momentary assessment of cognitive load during role transitions.      Second, the cross-sectional, between-person design of this study is poorly suited to capturing the dynamic processes through which executive functioning may influence spillover. Work-family spillover unfolds within days and across role transitions; an individual’s average level of executive functioning assessed at a single time point may bear little resemblance to the momentary cognitive resource fluctuations that shape whether a difficult workday spills over into a tense evening at home. Within-person diary designs offer a particularly promising alternative. Daily diary or experience sampling studies could assess morning cognitive functioning (e.g., via brief mobile assessments of working memory or attentional control), daily work and family demands, and end-of-day spillover, allowing researchers to test whether within-person days when an individual experiences greater cognitive depletion are also days when spillover is more pronounced. This approach would disentangle whether executive functioning matters for spillover as a stable individual difference—which the present study suggests it does not—or as a fluctuating daily resource, which remains an open question. Such designs could also capture the temporal dynamics of spillover, such as whether cognitive depletion at the end of the workday predicts negative mood or conflict during the evening, and whether recovery processes (e.g., sleep, leisure) restore cognitive resources that buffer against spillover the following day.      Third, the null findings in this study may partly reflect the limitations of quantitative survey methods for understanding a phenomenon that is experientially rich and context-dependent. Qualitative research designs could provide critical insight into the specific incidents and processes through which executive functioning difficulties lead to spillover in everyday life. Critical incident interviews with individuals who have diagnosed executive functioning difficulties (e.g., ADHD) or their partners could illuminate the concrete moments—the forgotten school pickup, the inability to disengage from a work problem during family time, the emotional outburst triggered by accumulated cognitive overload—that quantitative spillover scales may aggregate away. Such research could identify the specific executive processes most relevant to boundary management failures, inform the development of more ecologically valid measures, and generate hypotheses about when and for whom executive functioning is most consequential for spillover. Qualitative approaches could also surface compensatory strategies that individuals with executive functioning challenges use to manage spillover, which may obscure the EF–spillover relationship in cross-sectional quantitative designs if those strategies are effective.      Fourth, the sample was restricted to dual-earner couples, which limits generalizability to single-earner households, single parents, or individuals without partners. The dual-earner restriction also means the sample is relatively advantaged in terms of household income (M = $102,350 in MIDUS II; $132,684 in MIDUS III), which may attenuate effects if financial stress is a mechanism through which cognitive limitations influence spillover. Additionally, the MIDUS sample is predominantly White and middle-aged, limiting generalizability to more diverse populations.      Fifth, listwise deletion resulted in analytic samples substantially reduced from the full MIDUS samples. The analytic samples of 932 (MIDUS II) and 507 (MIDUS III) provided adequate power to detect standardized coefficients of ? ≥ .08 and ? ≥ .11, respectively. Given that the observed effects were consistently smaller than these thresholds, the null findings are unlikely to be attributable to insufficient power alone, though it remains possible that very small true effects exist below the study’s detection threshold.      Sixth, all spillover measures were self-report, raising the possibility that shared method variance or differences in self-insight could influence the observed relationships. Individuals with lower executive functioning may also have less accurate self-perceptions of their spillover experiences, which could attenuate observed associations. Multi-source designs incorporating partner or supervisor reports of spillover behaviors could address this limitation.      The examination of boundary conditions in this study was intended to shed light on the role that one’s work and home environment play in enhancing positive spillover and mitigating negative spillover. While the moderation hypotheses were not supported, the consistent main effects of social support across models and waves provide clear evidence that these environmental factors matter. Beyond the qualitative and within-person approaches described above, future research should also consider examining specific facets of executive functioning (e.g., inhibitory control, working memory updating) rather than the composite; investigating whether executive functioning moderates the effectiveness of boundary management strategies rather than directly predicting spillover; and examining populations with clinically significant executive dysfunction (e.g., individuals with ADHD) where effects may be more pronounced than in a community sample with a relatively restricted range of functioning.Conclusion      This study represents, to my knowledge, the first direct empirical examination of the relationship between executive functioning and work-family spillover. Despite strong theoretical rationale for expecting executive functioning to predict spillover outcomes, the results across two independent samples, 40 primary models, and comprehensive multiverse analyses indicate that the general executive functioning composite measured by the BTACT does not meaningfully predict negative or positive spillover between work and family domains. Gender, coworker support, and spousal support did not moderate this null relationship, and the few significant effects involving supervisor support failed to replicate across waves. The findings underscore the primacy of environmental factors—particularly job demands, family demands, and social support—in shaping work-family spillover experiences. By understanding these factors, organizations can develop targeted interventions and support systems to help employees maintain a healthy work-life balance, reduce turnover and absenteeism, and enhance overall employee engagement and job satisfaction, ultimately contributing to a healthier and more productive workforce. While the null findings for executive functioning do not preclude the possibility that more specific or proximally measured cognitive processes relate to spillover, they provide an important corrective to the assumption that broad cognitive ability differences are a primary driver of work-family outcomes.

APPENDIX      Multiverse Analyses      To assess the robustness of the primary findings, a multiverse analysis was conducted in which the executive functioning–spillover relationship was estimated across several alternative analytic specifications. These specifications were organized into four arms: (A) covariate variations (no covariates, demographics only, education as SES indicator); (B) covariate ? EF interactions (age, education, income, job demands, and family demands each interacted with EF, for all four spillover outcomes); (C) dual-earner couples (restricting the sample to households where both the respondent and spouse were employed, running all 20 regression models as a robustness check); and (D) model-set specific complete-case samples. Results are presented in Tables A1 through A4, with cross-wave replicability assessed in Table A5.      In MIDUS 2 (Tables A1 and A2), when no covariates were included, executive functioning was positively related to both negative work-to-family spillover (B = 0.250, ? = 0.084, p < .001) and negative family-to-work spillover (B = 0.246, ? = 0.094, p < .001). However, these effects disappeared once any control variables were introduced. In the covariate interaction arm, the Age ? EF interaction was significant for positive work-to-family spillover (B = ?0.018, p < .05), and the Family Demands ? EF interaction was significant for negative family-to-work spillover (B = 0.007, p < .05). In the dual-earner arm, EF was significantly related to positive family-to-work spillover (B = ?0.222, p < .05), and the EF ? Supervisor Support interaction was significant for positive family-to-work spillover (B = ?0.085, p < .05). The EF ? Coworker Support interaction was significant for negative work-to-family spillover in the dual-earner subsample (B = 0.122, p < .05), consistent with the primary analysis finding.      In MIDUS 3 (Tables A3 and A4), the no-covariate models again showed significant positive associations between executive functioning and negative work-to-family spillover (B = 0.538, ? = 0.119, p < .001) and negative family-to-work spillover (B = 0.253, ? = 0.069, p < .01). Several covariate ? EF interactions reached significance, including Job Demands ? EF for negative work-to-family spillover and negative family-to-work spillover. In the dual-earner arm, the EF ? Supervisor Support interaction was significant for positive work-to-family spillover (B = 0.235, p < .001), though the direction differed from MIDUS 2. The EF ? Gender interaction was significant for positive family-to-work spillover in the dual-earner subsample (B = ?1.132, p < .05).      Cross-wave replicability of the multiverse results (Table A5) was evaluated using the same OSC (2015) criteria applied to the primary analyses. Of the nine specifications that produced significant effects in MIDUS 2, four showed the same sign in MIDUS 3 (44.4%), three reached significance with the same sign in the second wave (33.3%), and of the four effects that were significant in both waves, three had overlapping confidence intervals (75.0%). Notably, the no-covariate models for negative work-to-family and negative family-to-work spillover met all three replicability criteria, as did the Job Demands ? EF interaction for negative family-to-work spillover. These results suggest that the positive zero-order association between executive functioning and negative spillover is replicable but is fully attenuated by the inclusion of covariates, reinforcing the primary finding that executive functioning does not have a robust or unique relationship with work-family spillover.      Power Analysis      Because sample size was determined by the existing MIDUS dataset rather than a priori power calculations, a sensitivity power analysis was conducted to determine the minimum detectable effect sizes and statistical power across the analytic samples (Tables A13 and A14). At 80% power with ? = .05, the MIDUS II sample (n = 1,289) could detect a minimum effect of f? = .011 (?R? = .011) for main effect models and f? = .012 (?R? = .012) for two-way interaction models, both well below Cohen's (1988) threshold for a small effect (f? = .02). The MIDUS III sample (n = 729) required somewhat larger effects for adequate power: f? = .020 (?R? = .020) for main effects and f? = .022 (?R? = .021) for two-way interactions, approximating the small effect benchmark. Expressed in terms of standardized regression coefficients (Tables A15–A20), the MIDUS II sample had 80% power to detect coefficients of approximately ? = .10 or larger across main effect and interaction models (assuming R?? = .30 for the focal predictor), whereas the MIDUS III sample required standardized coefficients of approximately ? = .12 or larger to achieve comparable power. At a small conventional effect size of f? = .02, MIDUS II provided 97.8% power for main effects and 96.6% for two-way interactions, while MIDUS III provided 80.3% and 75.9%, respectively. Both samples were well-powered (> 99%) to detect effects of f? = .05 or larger. These results indicate that MIDUS II had excellent power to detect even small effects, while MIDUS III had adequate power for main effects at the small benchmark but was slightly underpowered for interaction terms at that threshold.      Confirmatory Factor Analysis and Measurement Invariance      To evaluate the measurement structure of the work-family spillover items, confirmatoryfactor analyses were conducted specifying a four-factor model corresponding to the fourspillover dimensions: negative work-to-family, negative family-to-work, positivework-to-family, and positive family-to-work. Each factor was indicated by four items, fora total of 16 observed indicators. Model fit indices are presented in Table A6, withstandardized factor loadings and latent factor correlations reported in Tables A7 throughA10.      In MIDUS 2, the four-factor model yielded a significant chi-square test, ??(98) = 1769.25,p < .001, which is expected given the large sample size. Approximate fit indices indicated marginal model fit: CFI = .839, TLI = .803, RMSEA = .089, 90% CI [.086, .093], SRMR = .094. Although these values fell below conventional thresholds for good fit (CFI > .95, RMSEA < .06; Hu & Bentler, 1999), the four-factor structure was retained given strong theoretical justification and the well-established use of these scales in MIDUS research. Standardized factor loadings (Table A7) ranged from .306 to .905, with the majority exceeding .50. The weakest loading was observed for item B1SF27N on the positive family-to-work factor (? = .306), while the strongest was item B1SF27O on the same factor (? = .905). Latent factor correlations (Table A8) revealed a strong positive association between the two negative spillover dimensions (r = .639, p < .001) and a moderate positive association between the two positive spillover dimensions (r = .387, p < .001).      Cross-valence correlations were weak, with negative work-to-family spillover showing asmall negative correlation with positive family-to-work spillover (r = ?.124, p < .001).In MIDUS 3, the four-factor model showed a similar pattern, ??(98) = 1061.28, p < .001,with slightly improved fit relative to MIDUS 2: CFI = .865, TLI = .835, RMSEA = .085, 90%CI [.081, .090], SRMR = .091. Standardized factor loadings (Table A9) ranged from .237 to.863, again with the positive family-to-work item C1SF40N showing the weakest loading (? =.237) and C1SF40O the strongest (? = .863). Latent factor correlations (Table A10) wereconsistent with MIDUS 2: the two negative spillover dimensions were strongly correlated (r= .670, p < .001), and the two positive dimensions were moderately correlated (r = .495,p < .001). The negative association between negative family-to-work and positivefamily-to-work spillover was somewhat stronger in MIDUS 3 (r = ?.292, p < .001) than inMIDUS 2 (r = ?.165, p < .001).      Measurement invariance across gender was evaluated using a sequential model comparison approach (Tables A11 and A12). In MIDUS 2, configural invariance was established as the baseline model, CFI = .834, RMSEA = .091. Metric invariance (equal factor loadings) was supported, ?CFI = ?.002, ?RMSEA = ?.002, meeting Chen's (2007) criterion of ?CFI < .010. Scalar invariance (equal intercepts) was also supported, ?CFI = ?.009, ?RMSEA < .001, as was strict invariance (equal residual variances), ?CFI = ?.005, ?RMSEA = ?.002. In MIDUS 3, the same pattern held: metric invariance (?CFI = ?.002), scalar invariance (?CFI = ?.007), and strict invariance (?CFI = ?.003) were all supported.
